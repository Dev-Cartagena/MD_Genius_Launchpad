## Learning objectives

- Describe how pre-seed and seed rounds are usually structured, and who invests at each stage.
- Explain how a post-money SAFE works, including the valuation cap, the discount and the most-favored-nation variant.
- Calculate founder dilution across SAFEs and a later priced round, including an option pool increase.
- Decide when to raise and how much, based on the milestone the money must reach.

## Overview

Fundraising is a tool, not a goal. Money buys time to reach the next milestone that makes your company more valuable. Raise too early and you give up ownership for little progress; too late and you negotiate from weakness. Investors do this every week and you do it a few times in your life, so learn the mechanics first. This lesson covers general practice as of 2026. It is not legal, tax or financial advice. Terms change, rules differ by country, and every founder should have their own startup lawyer review documents before signing.

## How early rounds work

### The stages

Early rounds have loose names that overlap.

- Pre-seed: often the first outside money, raised on a team, an insight and early evidence. It typically comes from angels, accelerators and small pre-seed funds.
- Seed: raised once there is a product and early traction, such as paying customers or strong usage. It often comes from seed funds and angels, sometimes with one investor leading.
- Series A: usually a priced round led by a venture firm, raised once the company has shown it can grow in a repeatable way.

As of 2026, round sizes vary widely by country, sector and market cycle, and AI companies have often raised larger early rounds than other software startups. As a general norm, many founders aim to sell somewhere around 10 to 25% of the company in each early round. They are rough reference points, not rules: check current data for your market.

### Who invests

Angels invest their own money, and the best bring industry knowledge and introductions. Seed funds invest other people's money and need a few companies to return the whole fund, so they look for companies that could become very large. Strategic investors, such as corporations in your industry, can open doors but may scare off other buyers or investors. Choose investors as long-term partners.

## The post-money SAFE

Y Combinator created the SAFE, a simple agreement for future equity, in 2013, and moved to the post-money SAFE in 2018. Its documents are published free on YC's website and are widely used for pre-seed and seed rounds, especially in the United States.

### What a SAFE is

A SAFE is not equity yet and not debt. The investor pays money now. In exchange, they get the right to receive shares later, usually when the company raises a priced equity round. There is no interest rate and no maturity date. If the company is sold or dissolves before a priced round, the document sets out what the investor receives.

### Cap, discount and MFN

YC publishes a few standard versions.

- Valuation cap, no discount: the investor converts at the price implied by the cap, or at the priced round's price if that is lower.
- Discount, no cap: the investor converts at a discount to the priced round's price.
- Uncapped MFN: no cap and no discount, but if the company later issues SAFEs with better terms, this investor can take those terms.
- Pro rata side letter: an optional add-on that gives an investor the right to buy into a future round to maintain their ownership.

### Why "post-money" matters

The post-money cap is the company's value after all the SAFE money is counted. For a cap SAFE that converts at its cap, ownership is easy to calculate: the investment divided by the post-money cap. If the priced round's price is lower than the cap price, the holder converts at that price and gets more. For discount and MFN SAFEs, ownership is set only at the priced round. A $500,000 SAFE at a $10 million post-money cap buys 5% of the company, measured just before the priced round. YC's documents page notes that the cap includes all SAFE money raised but does not include a new or larger option pool created in the priced round. That means the pool increase dilutes SAFE holders as well as founders. Each extra SAFE dilutes the founders, not earlier SAFE holders, so keep a running total.

## Dilution: a worked example

Here is a simplified example. Real documents include details this example leaves out, so use a cap table tool and your lawyer.

### Starting point

Two founders own 90% of the company, 45% each. An option pool for future employees holds the other 10%.

### Step 1: two SAFEs

- Angels invest $250,000 on a SAFE with a $5 million post-money cap. That is 250,000 ÷ 5,000,000 = 5%.
- A pre-seed fund invests $750,000 on a SAFE with a $7.5 million post-money cap. That is 750,000 ÷ 7,500,000 = 10%.

Together the SAFE holders own 15%. Everyone else shares the remaining 85%. The founders go from 90% to 90% × 0.85 = 76.5%, and the pool goes from 10% to 8.5%.

### Step 2: a priced seed round

Later the company raises $3 million at a $12 million pre-money valuation, which is a $15 million post-money valuation. The new investors own 3 ÷ 15 = 20%. They also ask that the option pool equal 10% after the round. Because the pool increase is set up before the new money comes in, it dilutes existing holders, not the new investors.

After the round, new investors hold 20% and the pool holds 10%. The remaining 70% is split between the founders and the SAFE holders in the same proportions they had after step 1 (76.5 to 5 to 10). The result, rounded:

- Founders: 58.5%
- Angel SAFE: 3.8%
- Pre-seed fund SAFE: 7.7%
- Option pool: 10%
- Seed investors: 20%

The founders went from 90% to 58.5% across two rounds and still control the company. Dilution is not the enemy. Know the numbers before you agree, and make every round buy real progress.

## When to raise and how much

### When

Raise when you can show evidence that makes the next stage believable, and when money would clearly speed you up. Paul Graham's essay "Default Alive or Default Dead?" asks whether your company would reach profitability on its current money and growth. If so, you can raise from strength. If not, start early, because fundraising usually takes longer than expected.

### How much

Work from a milestone, not a valuation. Name the milestone that will justify the next round, such as a revenue level or proof of a repeatable sales motion. Estimate what it costs to get there, and add a buffer. Common advice is to raise enough for about 18 to 24 months of runway, but your plan should drive the number. Raising far more than you need usually means selling more of the company and spending loosely.

### Run a tight process

Paul Graham's "How to Raise Money" advises breadth-first search weighted by expected value: talk to many investors in parallel, focus on those most likely to invest, and close committed money as you get it. Keep it short: fundraising pulls founders away from customers.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana and Tomás are raising a pre-seed round on post-money SAFEs. They have two paying contractors, a pipeline of about 80 targets and a working association channel (founders' own data from week 7). Their milestone is to show that founder-led sales repeats: enough paying contractors in their region, with low churn, to justify a seed round.

Their plan estimates about 18 months of runway for $1 million. They use the structure in the worked example: $250,000 from two angels who know HVAC distribution at a $5 million post-money cap, and $750,000 from a pre-seed fund at a $7.5 million post-money cap. On their cap table, that sells 15% and takes the founders from 90% to 76.5%. All figures here are the founders' assumptions for a fictional company, not market benchmarks. They will have a startup lawyer review the SAFEs and the side letter before anyone signs.

## Common mistakes

- Signing SAFEs one at a time without tracking total dilution on a cap table.
- Treating a SAFE as free money because it has no valuation in the headline.
- Raising an amount chosen by gut feel instead of by the milestone it must reach.
- Running a slow, serial process and letting investor interest go cold.
- Signing documents without a lawyer reviewing them.

## Key takeaways

- Fundraising buys time to reach a milestone. Pick the milestone first, then the amount.
- A cap SAFE converting at its cap gives investment ÷ post-money cap, measured before the priced round.
- Every extra SAFE dilutes the founders, and a pool increase in the priced round dilutes SAFE holders and founders.
- Model dilution on a cap table before you agree to terms.
- Norms change. Treat round sizes and dilution ranges as rough guides as of 2026, and always use a lawyer.
