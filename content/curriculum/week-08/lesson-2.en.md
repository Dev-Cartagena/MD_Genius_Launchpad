## Learning objectives

- Write a one-sentence description of your company that a stranger understands on first hearing.
- Structure a seed deck around a clear narrative arc, using the classic slide order.
- Present traction honestly, with the metrics investors use to judge early companies.
- State a specific ask: the amount, the instrument and the milestone the money will reach.

## Overview

A deck does not raise money by itself, but a confusing deck can lose a round. Investors see many pitches each week and decide quickly whether to look closer. Paul Graham's essay "How to Convince Investors" says investors want three things: formidable founders, a promising market and, usually, some evidence of success so far. Your deck and your pitch exist to show those three things as clearly and quickly as possible. For an AI startup there is an extra burden. Investors have seen many products built on the same foundation models, so you must show why your company is more than a thin layer that a competitor or the model provider could copy.

## Start with clarity

### The one-liner

Before you design a single slide, write one sentence that says what you do, for whom. Paul Graham's essay "How to Present to Investors", written with YC's Demo Day in mind, puts the first goal plainly: explain what you are doing. Avoid abstract words like "platform", "revolutionize" and "AI-powered solution". Name the user, the job and the result. "We help mid-size HVAC contractors fix equipment on the first visit with a voice assistant for their technicians" is clear. "An AI-native operating system for the trades" is not.

Test the one-liner on people outside your industry. If they cannot repeat it back accurately, rewrite it.

### Answer the obvious questions

Michael Seibel's YC library article "How to Pitch Your Company" reduces the pitch to answering a short set of plain questions in plain language. Whatever form you use, an investor finishing your pitch should be able to say what you do, who it is for, why now, what progress you have made, why your team, and what you are asking for.

## The classic seed-deck structure

There is no single correct deck, but most strong seed decks follow a similar order. YC's library article "How to build your seed round pitch deck" lays out its own template; the structure below reflects common practice. Aim for about 10 to 12 slides, one idea per slide, with large text and few words.

1. Title and one-liner: company name and the sentence above.
2. Problem: who has the problem, how often and what it costs them. Use your customers' words and numbers.
3. Solution: what the product does, ideally shown with a screenshot or a short demo, not described.
4. Why now: the change in technology, behavior or regulation that makes this possible today.
5. Traction: the strongest evidence that people want it, with dates.
6. Market: a bottom-up estimate from week 4, showing the beachhead and how it expands.
7. Business model: how you charge and what a customer is worth.
8. Competition and insight: the alternatives, including doing nothing, and the secret you understand that others do not.
9. Team: why these founders are the right people, with specific evidence.
10. The ask: how much you are raising, on what instrument and what milestone it reaches.

Keep a separate appendix for detail: unit economics, a product roadmap, your evaluation results and the cap table. Use it to answer questions, not in the main flow.

### A narrative arc, not a list

The order matters because it tells a story. The problem creates tension. The solution and the why-now resolve it. Traction proves that the resolution is real. Market and model show how big it can get. Team and ask explain why you, and why now. When a slide does not move the story forward, cut it or move it to the appendix. Many founders find it useful to write the narration as a paragraph per slide before designing anything.

### Showing the AI moat

For AI companies, the competition slide should address the question every investor has: what stops a well-funded competitor, or the model provider itself, from doing this? Good answers come from week 1 and week 5: proprietary data that improves with use, deep embedding in a workflow, distribution advantages, and an evaluation system that proves your quality on real tasks. "We use the best model" is not a moat, because anyone can use it.

## Framing traction honestly

Traction is usually the slide that matters most at seed. Present it in a way that is impressive and fully defensible.

### What to show

- Revenue, if you have it: monthly or annual recurring revenue and its growth rate.
- Paying customers and pilots, clearly separated. Never count an unpaid pilot as a customer.
- Retention or engagement: weekly active users as a share of total, or cohort retention from week 6.
- Leading indicators: pipeline, conversion from pilot to paid, referral rates.
- For AI products, quality and cost: accuracy on your evaluation set and gross margin after inference costs.

### How to show it

Show trends over time, not single numbers. A chart of weekly usage rising for ten weeks says more than a total. Label every number with its date and its definition. Avoid vanity metrics like total sign-ups or page views if they do not lead to revenue. If your numbers are small, show growth rate and engagement depth, and explain what drives them. Investors will check your claims in diligence, so anything you exaggerate now will cost you trust later.

## The ask

End with a specific ask. Say how much you are raising, the instrument, for example a post-money SAFE, and the milestone the money reaches, such as a level of recurring revenue or a repeatable sales motion by a given date. Some founders include the valuation cap. Others discuss it only in meetings. Either can work, but decide in advance and be consistent. Add a simple use-of-funds line, such as hiring, product and go-to-market, tied to the milestone.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana and Tomás wrote their one-liner first: "Fieldnote helps mid-size HVAC contractors fix equipment on the first visit, with a voice assistant that diagnoses problems, finds parts and files reports for their technicians." They tested it on five people outside the trade and rewrote it twice.

Their deck has eleven slides. The problem slide uses a quote from an operations manager and the founders' estimate of what a callback costs, labeled as their own estimate. The solution slide is a 20-second clip of a technician asking about an error code on a rooftop unit. Why now: speech recognition that works on noisy job sites, and language models that can answer from long equipment manuals, both much better than a few years ago.

The traction slide separates paying customers from pilots and shows dates. It includes the 46% "very disappointed" result from their product-market-fit survey, their pilot-to-paid conversion and the association channel results from week 7, all labeled as founders' own data. Their competition slide compares field-service-management suites, paper manuals and calling a senior technician, and states their insight: the hard part is not the model but grounding answers in manufacturer manuals and parts catalogs, scored against an evaluation set of 120 real diagnostic questions that grows with every job.

The ask slide reads: raising $1 million on post-money SAFEs to reach a repeatable regional sales motion within about 18 months. They leave the valuation cap off the slide and discuss it in meetings.

## Common mistakes

- A vague one-liner full of buzzwords that leaves investors guessing what you do.
- Counting unpaid pilots or sign-ups as customers.
- Too many slides with too much text, so the story gets lost.
- No answer to "what stops the model provider from doing this?"
- An ask with no milestone, so investors cannot judge whether the amount makes sense.

## Your turn

Take 30 minutes. Write your one-liner and test it on two people outside your industry. Then write one sentence of narration for each of the ten slides in the classic structure. Mark the weakest slide and write down what evidence would make it stronger.

## Key takeaways

- Investors look for formidable founders, a promising market and evidence of progress. Show all three quickly.
- Start with a one-liner that names the user, the job and the result.
- Follow the classic order so the deck tells a story: problem, solution, why now, traction, market, model, competition, team, ask.
- Present traction as trends, with dates and definitions, and separate paid customers from pilots.
- End with a specific ask tied to a milestone.
