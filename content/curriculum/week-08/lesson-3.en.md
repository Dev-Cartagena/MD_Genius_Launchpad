## Learning objectives

- Write and deliver a two-minute pitch that explains what you do, why it matters and why your team will win.
- Build and prioritize an investor target list, and run first meetings that lead to a clear next step.
- Follow up, manage diligence and close committed money without letting the process drag.
- Write a monthly investor update that keeps current and future investors informed and helpful.

## Overview

Demo Day is a starting gun, not a finish line. Two minutes on stage, or on a call, can open dozens of conversations, but the round closes in the weeks that follow, through meetings, follow-up and signed documents. The founders who raise well treat this period as a short, intense sales process with a target list, a pipeline and deadlines, just like the founder-led sales you ran in week 7. This lesson covers the pitch itself, the meetings after it, how to close, and the habit that keeps investors on your side for years: a regular investor update.

## The two-minute pitch

### What two minutes can do

Two minutes is about 250 to 300 spoken words. That is enough for one clear idea and a few pieces of evidence, not your whole deck. The goal is not to close anyone on stage. It is to make the right investors want a meeting. Paul Graham's essay "How to Present to Investors", written with YC's Demo Day in mind, gives two goals: explain what you are doing, and explain why users will want it. Everything else is supporting detail.

### A simple structure

1. What you do, 10 to 15 seconds: your one-liner from lesson 2.
2. Your strongest number, 15 seconds: lead with traction if you have it, such as revenue, growth rate or paying customers.
3. The problem, 20 seconds: one concrete customer story with a cost.
4. How it works, 20 seconds: what the product does, and a demo or screenshot if the format allows.
5. Why you win, 20 seconds: your insight or moat, and why this team.
6. Market, 15 seconds: the beachhead and where it leads.
7. The ask, 10 seconds: how much you are raising and how to reach you.

### Delivery

Write the script word for word, then rehearse it out loud until you can deliver it calmly without reading. Time every run. Cut words until you finish at 1:50, which leaves room for nerves. Use one number per sentence at most, and say numbers slowly. Practice in front of people who do not know your company and ask them to repeat back what you do. If they cannot, fix the script, not the delivery.

## Investor meetings

### Build a target list

Make a list of 40 to 80 investors who invest at your stage, in your sector and in your geography, and who have written checks recently. Note who could lead and who would only follow. Rank them by fit and likelihood. Warm introductions from founders an investor has backed usually work better than cold email, but a short, specific cold email with strong traction can work too. Track every investor in a pipeline with stages, as you did with customers in week 7.

### Run the process in parallel

Paul Graham's "How to Raise Money" recommends breadth-first search weighted by expected value: meet many investors in a compressed period and spend most of your time on the ones most likely to invest. His essay "Investor Herd Dynamics" explains why: investors are influenced by each other, and interest from other investors creates a deadline. A slow, one-at-a-time process loses that momentum.

### The first meeting

The goal of a first meeting is a clear next step, such as a partner meeting, a diligence call or a decision date. Open with your one-liner and your strongest number, then let the investor steer with questions. Expect questions about why now, competition, the moat for an AI product, unit economics and how you will use the money. Answer directly. If you do not know, say so and follow up. Before you leave, ask about their process: who decides, how long it takes and what they would need to see.

You are also choosing them. Ask founders they have backed what the investor is like when things go badly.

## Follow-up and closing

### Follow up within a day

Send a short thank-you within 24 hours, with answers to anything you promised and the agreed next step. Keep a light data room ready: the deck, a metrics summary, a simple financial model, the cap table and customer references. Share it when an investor is seriously engaged, not with everyone.

### Closing committed money

A verbal "we're in" is not money in the bank. Paul Graham's advice in "How to Raise Money" is to close committed money: get the documents signed and the funds wired as soon as an investor commits. With SAFEs you can close investors one at a time, which his essay "A Fundraising Survival Guide" describes as a rolling close. Set a target amount and a realistic deadline, and tell investors about both honestly. Use your lawyer to prepare and review the documents and any side letters. Do not sign terms you have not had reviewed.

### When to stop

Stop when you have raised what your plan needs, or when the process has run its planned course. Fundraising takes founders away from customers. If the round is not coming together, ask the investors who said no what would change their mind, and decide whether to adjust the amount, change the target list, or go back to building traction and try again later.

## Investor updates

A regular update, usually monthly, keeps investors informed and makes it easy for them to help. It also builds trust with investors who passed, some of whom may invest in your next round. Keep the format the same every month so readers can compare.

1. Key metrics: the same few numbers every month, such as revenue, customers, burn and runway.
2. Highlights: two or three wins.
3. Lowlights: what went wrong and what you are doing about it. Investors trust founders who share bad news early.
4. Asks: specific requests, such as introductions to named companies or help hiring a role.

Put the asks near the top if they are urgent. Keep it short enough to read in two minutes.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana delivered Fieldnote's Demo Day pitch. Her script opened with the one-liner, then: "Two paying contractors and a pipeline of eighty, all from founder-led sales in one region" (founders' own data). Next came a 15-second story about a technician who fixed a rooftop unit on the first visit using Fieldnote, the insight about grounding answers in manufacturer manuals, Tomás's background in machine learning and the ask: $1 million on post-money SAFEs. They rehearsed it more than twenty times and finished at 1:50.

Their target list had 50 investors, tiered by fit, including angels from HVAC distribution and pre-seed funds that back vertical AI. They booked first meetings in a three-week window. When a pre-seed fund committed, they sent the SAFE through their lawyer the same day and asked for the wire that week. They used a rolling close and told every investor the target amount and the date they planned to stop.

After closing, Tomás set up a monthly update: paying contractors, active technicians, first-time-fix improvement in pilots, gross margin, burn and runway, then highlights, lowlights and two asks. The first update asked for introductions to three named contractors in a neighboring region.

## Common mistakes

- Trying to squeeze the whole deck into two minutes instead of one idea and strong evidence.
- Meeting investors one at a time over months, so momentum never builds.
- Treating a verbal yes as closed and not getting documents signed and money wired.
- Sharing a full data room with everyone, or with no one until it is too late.
- Stopping investor updates after the round closes, or sending only good news.

## Your turn

Take 30 minutes. Write your two-minute pitch word for word using the seven-part structure. Read it aloud with a timer and cut until it runs at 1:50. Then list ten investors who invest at your stage and sector, and write one line on why each is a fit.

## Key takeaways

- The two-minute pitch should make the right investors want a meeting: explain what you do and why users want it.
- Run fundraising like a sales process: a target list, a pipeline, parallel meetings and deadlines.
- Every first meeting should end with a clear next step and an understanding of the investor's process.
- Close committed money quickly, with documents reviewed by your lawyer.
- Send a monthly update with metrics, highlights, lowlights and asks, in the same format every time.
