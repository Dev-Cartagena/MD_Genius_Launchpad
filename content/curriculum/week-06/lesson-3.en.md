## Learning objectives

- Set a price from the value you create, using your costs as a floor, not a formula.
- Compare per-seat, usage, outcome and hybrid pricing for an AI product.
- Write a pricing hypothesis you can test with real buyers in two to four weeks.
- Structure a pilot so it ends in a paid contract instead of an open-ended trial.

## Overview

Pricing is one of the most powerful levers a startup has and one of the least practiced. Founders often pick a number in an afternoon, usually too low, and never revisit it. AI products add two complications. First, they often replace part of someone's work, so the value can be far larger than a typical software subscription. Second, they carry real costs per use, so a price that ignores usage can lose money on your best customers. This lesson treats price as a hypothesis you test, like everything else in this program.

## Price on value, not on cost

### The pricing thermometer

In his YC talk "Startup Pricing 101", Kevin Hale describes price as sitting between two other numbers: your cost to deliver and the value the customer perceives. The gap between cost and price is your margin, the reason you want to sell. The gap between price and value is the customer's reason to buy. Hale's most repeated point is that founders consistently undercharge.

### Quantify the value

Before you choose a number, write down the value in the customer's own terms. Common forms are hours saved, revenue gained, costs avoided and risk reduced. Use the evidence you collected in customer discovery and pilots, not guesses. For a team of ten analysts each saving four hours a week, value is those hours at their loaded cost, plus whatever the faster output enables.

### Cost is the floor

Your cost model from Lesson 2 sets the minimum. If a price does not cover inference, review and support with a healthy gross margin at heavy usage, it is not a real option. Bessemer Venture Partners' 2026 "AI pricing and monetization playbook" warns against cost-plus pricing, such as doubling your costs, precisely because it ignores value. Use costs to rule out bad prices, and value to choose a good one.

## Choosing a pricing model

Most AI products use one of four models, or a mix.

### Per seat

A fixed fee per user per month. Buyers understand it and can budget for it, and revenue is predictable. The risk for AI is that a seat price stays flat while usage and cost per seat vary widely. And if your product makes each worker much more productive, customers may need fewer seats over time.

### Usage-based

A charge per unit consumed: per request, per document, per minute of audio. Revenue tracks cost, which protects margins. But buyers who are not technical find usage hard to budget, and some users ration a tool when they feel a meter running, which hurts adoption exactly where you need it.

### Outcome-based

A charge per successful result, such as a resolved support ticket or a completed claim. Bessemer's playbook cites Intercom's Fin AI agent, priced per resolution, as a prominent example. Outcome pricing aligns best with value, but it needs a clear, measurable definition of success that both sides trust, and you absorb the cost of failures.

### Hybrid

A base subscription that includes a usage allowance, with charges above it, or a platform fee plus outcome tiers. As of 2026 many AI companies use some form of hybrid because it gives buyers predictability and protects the seller's margin.

### How to choose your value metric

Whatever the model, the unit you charge for, your value metric, should pass five checks:

1. It grows when the customer gets more value.
2. The buyer can predict and budget for it.
3. It is easy to explain in one sentence.
4. You can measure it reliably.
5. It keeps gross margin healthy for your heaviest users.

## Writing and testing a pricing hypothesis

### The hypothesis

Write it like any other test: "We believe (buyer) will pay (price) per (value metric) because it delivers (value), and we will know we are right if (evidence) by (date)." Evidence should be commitment, not compliments: a signed order, a paid pilot, a budget approval.

### Testing it

Quote a real price in real sales conversations and watch the reaction. Bessemer's playbook suggests raising price until buyers hesitate, then stopping just before it blocks deals. If nobody pushes back, you are probably too cheap. If you have two candidate models, present both to similar buyers and record which they choose and why. Sample sizes will be small, so look for clear patterns rather than statistical proof.

## Pilots that turn into paid contracts

Many AI startups get stuck in "pilot purgatory": months of free trials that never convert. The fix is to design the pilot as the first stage of a contract.

### Make it paid

Even a modest fee changes behavior. A customer who pays assigns an owner, gives you access and shows up to reviews. If you discount, ask for something in return, such as a case study, a reference call or a longer term.

### Agree on success criteria up front

Before the pilot starts, write down two or three measurable outcomes, how they will be measured, the baseline, and the date of the decision. Use the customer's own data where you can, so the result is theirs, not yours.

### Pre-agree the conversion

State in the pilot agreement what happens if the criteria are met: the price, the term and the scope of the full contract. Name the person who signs. Then hold a formal review on the agreed date. A pilot without a decision date is a free trial with extra steps.

## Case study: Fieldnote

With 3 pilot contractors and a gross margin model from Lesson 2, Ana and Tomás tested two pricing models. Option A was per technician: a monthly fee per active technician, tested at 40 dollars. Option B was per job: a charge for each service visit where the assistant was used. All prices and results here are the founders' own hypothetical tests.

They presented both options to the operations manager at each pilot. Two of the three preferred per technician because it fit how they already budget for tools and trucks. Ana also heard a worry about per job: a manager said technicians might avoid using the assistant on simple calls to keep costs down, which is the usage rationing problem. The largest contractor asked whether price could depend on improvement in first-time-fix rate, the pain Fieldnote found in Week 3. That is an outcome model, attractive but hard to measure fairly so early.

Their pricing hypothesis: mid-size HVAC contractors will pay a per-technician monthly fee that includes a generous question allowance, with a fair-use limit that protects margin for the heaviest users, because it reduces repeat visits and parts errors. They will know they are right if at least two pilots convert at that price within 60 days. To get there, each pilot now has written success criteria based on the contractor's own first-time-fix data, a decision date and a pre-agreed annual price on conversion.

## Common mistakes

- Pricing from cost or from a competitor's page instead of from the customer's value.
- Charging a flat seat price while ignoring heavy users who make some accounts unprofitable.
- Picking a usage meter that makes users ration the product.
- Running free pilots with no success criteria, no decision date and no conversion price.
- Never raising prices because no one has complained.

## Your turn

Spend 20 minutes. Write the value your product creates for one customer, in their numbers. List two candidate pricing models and score each value metric against the five checks. Then write one pricing hypothesis using the template above, and draft three success criteria for your next pilot.

## Key takeaways

- Price sits between cost and value; use cost as the floor and value to set the number, and expect to be too cheap.
- Per seat, usage, outcome and hybrid each trade predictability against alignment with value and cost.
- A good value metric grows with customer value, is predictable and keeps margins healthy for heavy users.
- Treat price as a hypothesis tested with commitments, not compliments.
- Design pilots to convert: paid, with success criteria, a decision date and a pre-agreed price.
