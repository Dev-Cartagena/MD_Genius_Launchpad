## Goal

Write a one-page founder and idea memo that a mentor or investor could read in three minutes and understand who you are, what problem you are attacking, why your team and why now. The memo becomes the starting point for every later deliverable in this program, so write it to be revised, not to impress.

Estimated time: about 3 hours.

## Steps

1. Write your idea in one sentence: "We help [specific user] do [specific job] by [approach], which matters now because [specific change]."
2. Describe the problem in one paragraph. Name who has it, what they do about it today and what that costs them in time, money or risk. Use only what you have seen or heard directly, and mark anything that is a guess.
3. Run the tarpit check. List the four tarpit traits from lesson 1 and write one honest line on each for your idea. If your idea has been tried before, name one attempt and explain what is different now.
4. Write your why now in no more than two sentences, naming one specific change in technology, behavior, market structure or regulation, and the user it unlocks.
5. Draw your value map: place the product on the horizontal–vertical and copilot–agent axes from lesson 2, today and in two years. Write your moat hypothesis for data, workflow embedding and distribution, marking each as "have", "can build" or "unlikely".
6. Write the team section. For each founder, give one or two lines on insight, access and staying power, the areas they own and the time they commit. State the equity split, the vesting terms and how shared decisions are made. If you are a solo founder, describe the co-founder you are looking for.
7. List your three biggest open questions, the things that, if wrong, would most change the plan. These feed directly into next week's assumption map.
8. Fit it all on one page. Cut adjectives, keep facts.

## Deliverable

A one-page memo, as a PDF or a shared document, with these headings: Idea, Problem, Tarpit check, Why now, Value map and moat, Team, Open questions. Attach a photo or image of your value map if you drew it by hand.

## How it will be reviewed

- Specificity: the user, the job and the problem are concrete enough that a reviewer could find ten such people.
- Evidence: claims about the problem come from direct observation or conversation, and guesses are labeled as guesses.
- Why now: it names a specific change linked to the user, not a general trend.
- Moat realism: the moat hypothesis is honest about what you have and what you still need to build.
- Team: roles, decision rights, equity and vesting are written down and agreed by every founder.
- Clarity: it fits on one page and a reader can follow it in about three minutes.
