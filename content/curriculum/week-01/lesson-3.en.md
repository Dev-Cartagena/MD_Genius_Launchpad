## Learning objectives

- Assess co-founder fit using concrete evidence, not just friendship or shared excitement.
- Divide roles and decision rights so that every important decision has a clear owner.
- Explain how founder equity, vesting and cliffs work, and why they protect everyone.
- Set up a weekly operating cadence your team can keep for the next eight weeks.

## Overview

Investors back teams before they back products, especially at the earliest stage, when the product will change many times. Paul Graham listed having a single founder as the first item in his essay "The 18 Mistakes That Kill Startups", and YC partners often describe co-founder conflict as a frequent cause of early startup deaths. The good news is that most team failures are predictable and preventable. They come from unclear roles, unspoken expectations about equity and commitment, and no regular way to make decisions. This lesson gives you the structure to avoid them while things are still easy.

## Co-founder fit

### What you are looking for

A co-founder is not an early employee with a bigger title. It is the person you will make the hardest decisions of your working life with, often for many years. Look for:

- Shared values and ambition. Do you agree on how big you want this to be, how hard you will work and what you will not do to win?
- Complementary skills. Early AI startups usually need someone who can build the product and someone who can reach and sell to customers. Two people with the same skills often leave a dangerous gap.
- Trust under stress. You will disagree. The question is whether you can disagree openly and still move forward.
- Equal commitment. If one founder is full time and the other is "helping on weekends", you have a mismatch that will grow.

### How to test fit before you commit

The best evidence is having worked together before. YC's advice on finding a co-founder repeatedly favors people you already know well over strangers met at an event. If you have not worked together, create that history quickly:

1. Do a real project together for two to four weeks, with a deadline and something to ship.
2. Have the hard conversations early: money, time commitment, location, what happens if one of you wants to leave, and who would be CEO.
3. Talk to people who have worked with your potential co-founder, the same way you would check references for a senior hire.

YC runs a free Co-Founder Matching platform if you are still looking. Treat any match as the start of a trial, not a decision.

## Roles and decision making

### Dividing the work

Early roles should follow the work, not titles. A simple split many technical startups use is that one founder owns product and engineering, and the other owns customers, sales and operations. Write down who owns what, including the unglamorous areas such as finance, legal, hiring and investor updates.

### Who decides

Most decisions should be made by the person who owns that area, quickly, with input from the other founder. For the few decisions that are truly shared, such as raising money, hiring an executive or changing strategy, agree in advance how you will decide. Many teams name one founder as CEO with a final say after real discussion. That is not about status. It is about avoiding deadlock, which can stall a small company for weeks.

A useful habit is to label decisions as reversible or hard to reverse. Reversible decisions, like trying a new onboarding flow, should be made fast by the owner. Hard-to-reverse decisions, like signing a long contract or changing your target market, deserve a written proposal and a deliberate discussion.

## Equity and vesting

### Splitting equity

Michael Seibel of YC argues that co-founders should usually split equity equally, or very close to it. His reasoning is that building a valuable company takes many years, so small differences in who did what in the first months should not decide ownership for the next decade. A lopsided split can also signal to investors that one founder is not really a co-founder. Unequal splits are not always wrong, for example when one founder has worked full time for a year before the other joins, but you should be able to explain the reason clearly.

Harvard professor Noam Wasserman's book "The Founder's Dilemmas" is a useful deeper reading on how early decisions about splits, roles and control shape what happens later.

### Vesting and cliffs

Vesting means founders earn their shares over time instead of owning them all on day one. The most common structure in US startups is four years of vesting with a one-year cliff: nothing vests in the first year, a quarter vests at the one-year mark, and the rest vests monthly over the next three years. If a founder leaves early, the company can buy back the unvested shares.

Vesting protects everyone, including the founder who stays. Without it, a co-founder who leaves after three months could keep half the company, which makes the company very hard to fund. Seibel makes the same point in his advice: if you worry about what happens if you have to part ways, a proper vesting schedule is the answer.

### Paperwork to do early

Rules differ by country, so work with a startup lawyer. In general, as of 2026, a fundable company needs:

- A proper legal entity. Many teams that plan to raise from US investors use a Delaware C corporation.
- Founder stock agreements with vesting.
- Intellectual property assignment, so that the code, models and data belong to the company and not to individual founders.
- In the US, a timely 83(b) tax election when founders receive stock that vests. The filing window is short, generally 30 days, so ask your lawyer about it the day you incorporate.

## A weekly operating cadence

Small teams do not need much process, but they need a rhythm. A simple weekly cadence keeps founders aligned and makes progress visible.

### A proven minimum

1. Monday planning, 30 minutes. Review last week's goals, agree on the top three goals for this week, each with one owner and a result you can check.
2. A short daily check-in, 10 minutes or async. What did you finish, what is next, what is blocking you?
3. A metrics review once a week. Pick a small number of numbers that matter at your stage, such as customer conversations held, pilots active and weekly active users, and track them in one shared sheet.
4. A Friday retro, 20 minutes. What worked, what did not, what will you change? End with one honest question: "Is there anything we are not saying to each other?"

### Making it stick

Write goals as outcomes, not activities. "Talk to customers" is an activity. "Hold eight discovery calls with HVAC contractors" is an outcome. Keep one shared document with weekly goals and results. After eight weeks, it becomes a record of how fast you learn, which is exactly what mentors and investors want to see.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana and Tomás had worked together only on two weekend ride-alongs, so they ran a three-week trial before forming the company. They built a rough voice prototype, showed it to five technicians Ana knew and wrote a short summary together. The trial showed they argued productively. It also showed a gap. Neither of them enjoyed finance and legal, so they assigned it to Ana explicitly instead of letting it drift.

Their agreements:

- Roles: Ana owns customers, sales, operations and finance. Tomás owns product, engineering and data.
- CEO: Ana, with a final say on shared decisions after discussion. They agreed to revisit the arrangement after their first fundraise.
- Equity: an equal split, with four-year vesting and a one-year cliff for both, documented with a lawyer when they incorporated.
- Cadence: Monday planning, a daily async check-in in their team chat, and a Friday retro. Their first weekly goals were "eight conversations with contractors" for Ana and "a voice prototype that works in a noisy mechanical room" for Tomás.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing a co-founder because they are a friend or available, without testing how you work together.
- Delaying the equity and vesting conversation because it feels awkward. It only gets harder.
- Founders with no vesting, which scares investors and leaves the company exposed if someone leaves.
- Vague roles, so that two people own everything and nobody owns the boring parts.
- No regular rhythm, so problems surface only when they have become crises.

## Your turn

Take 30 minutes with your co-founder, or on your own if you are still looking. Write a one-page founders' agreement draft: who owns which areas, how shared decisions are made, the equity split you intend and why, the vesting terms and your weekly cadence. If you are a solo founder, write the profile of the co-founder you need and three places you will look for them this month.

## Key takeaways

- Test co-founder fit with real work and hard conversations before you commit.
- Every area needs one owner. Agree in advance how shared decisions are made, so you never deadlock.
- Equal or near-equal splits with vesting protect the company and every founder in it.
- Handle entity, IP assignment and vesting paperwork early, with a lawyer.
- A simple weekly cadence turns a group of founders into a team that ships.
