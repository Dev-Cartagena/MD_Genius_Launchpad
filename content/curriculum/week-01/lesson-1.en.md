## Learning objectives

- Explain what "make something people want" means in practice and how to test your idea against it.
- Tell a good startup idea from a tarpit idea using a short set of questions.
- Assess your own founder–market fit honestly and name what you still need to learn.
- Write a "why now" argument that points to a specific change in technology, behavior or regulation.

## Overview

Most startups do not fail because the team could not build the product. They fail because they built something nobody wanted badly enough to change their behavior. AI makes this risk bigger, not smaller. A capable team can ship an impressive demo in a weekend, so the bottleneck has moved from "can we build it" to "should anyone care". The first week of this program is about getting the starting point right: a real problem, founders with an unfair insight into it, and a reason it can be solved now and not five years ago.

## Make something people want

Y Combinator's motto is "Make something people want." It sounds obvious. In practice it is the rule founders break most often, because building is fun and finding out that nobody needs what you built is painful.

### What "want" really means

People want something when they are already trying to solve the problem with what they have. They are using spreadsheets, sending messages back and forth, paying a contractor, or putting up with a slow manual process. Real wanting shows up as behavior, not compliments. A friend saying "that's a cool idea" is not evidence. A stranger asking "when can I use it?" and then following up is.

Paul Graham's essay "How to Get Startup Ideas" describes the ideal early user base as a small group that wants the product urgently, rather than a large group that wants it a little. He uses the image of a well: you would rather have a narrow, deep hole than a wide, shallow one. A narrow group with an urgent need will forgive a rough product, tell you what is broken, and pull you forward.

### A quick test

Ask these four questions about your idea:

1. Who exactly has this problem? Name a job title, a company type, or a specific situation.
2. What do they do about it today, and what does that cost them in time, money or risk?
3. How would you know, within weeks, that they want your solution?
4. Would at least a handful of them use a rough first version every week?

If you cannot answer the first two concretely, you do not yet have an idea. You have a topic.

## Good ideas and tarpit ideas

### What a tarpit idea is

YC partners Dalton Caldwell and Michael Seibel use the term "tarpit idea" for an idea that looks easy and attractive, that many smart people have tried, and that keeps failing for structural reasons. Classic examples are apps for coordinating plans with friends, or generic recommendation engines for restaurants or music. The idea feels fresh to each new founder because the failures are invisible. The companies that died did not write press releases about it.

Tarpit ideas tend to share a few traits:

- The problem is real but mild. People complain about it but rarely pay to fix it.
- The idea relies on many people changing their habits at the same time.
- There is no clear buyer with a budget.
- The founder found it by brainstorming, not by living with the problem.

AI has created new tarpits. "A chatbot for X" and "a general AI assistant for everyone" are the friend-coordination apps of this decade. They demo well and retain poorly, because they are not tied to a specific, painful job.

### Signals of a good idea

Graham argues that the best ideas are usually noticed rather than invented. They come from founders who are "living in the future" of some field and see what is missing. Good ideas often look like:

- A boring, unglamorous problem that a specific group pays to solve today. Graham calls our tendency to ignore these "schlep blindness."
- A problem the founder has had personally or watched up close for years.
- Something that seems too small or too niche at first, but sits at the edge of a larger market.
- A problem where a recent change makes a new solution possible.

When you hear a tarpit warning, do not panic. Ask what has changed. YC partners have pointed out that a new technology can turn a former tarpit into a viable idea. The question is whether that change is real and specific, or whether you are hoping that "AI" will fix a demand problem. It will not.

## Founder–market fit

Founder–market fit is the idea that some founders are much better placed than others to build a particular company. It is not about credentials. It is about insight, access and staying power.

### Three questions to ask yourself

- Insight: do you understand the problem in a way outsiders do not? Can you describe the workflow, the vocabulary, the workarounds and the politics?
- Access: can you reach twenty potential customers this month without paid advertising?
- Staying power: will you still care about this problem in year five, when the work is tedious and the early excitement is gone?

Startups take a long time. YC partners often say that building a company of real value takes the better part of a decade. A founder who chose the space because it looked hot on social media will usually quit when it stops looking hot.

### What if you do not have it yet

Founder–market fit can be earned. You can work inside the industry for a few months, spend weeks shadowing users, or recruit a co-founder who has lived the problem. What you cannot do is skip it. If your team has no insider, closing that gap becomes the first milestone on your roadmap.

## Why now

Investors ask "why now?" because it separates ideas that were impossible yesterday from ideas that were simply ignored. A strong answer names a specific change and explains what it unlocks.

### Kinds of "why now"

- Technology: something became possible, cheap or good enough. In 2026 that includes speech recognition and generation that work in noisy, real-world settings, models that can read long technical documents, and capable phones in every worker's pocket.
- Behavior: users adopted a new habit that your product depends on, such as workers being comfortable talking to an assistant.
- Market structure: a wave of retirements, consolidation or labor shortages changes who buys and why.
- Regulation: a new rule creates a need, or a removed rule opens a market.

### Writing your answer

A weak "why now" says "AI is getting better." A strong one says "speech models now handle noisy job sites well enough, and every technician already carries a phone, so a hands-free assistant is usable for the first time." Notice that the strong version links the change directly to the product and to a specific user. Write yours in two sentences. If you need five, you probably have not found it.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana spent close to a decade in HVAC operations, most recently as operations lead at a regional contractor. She watched the same pattern every week. A technician arrives at a rooftop unit, cannot place the fault code, calls a senior colleague who is on another job, waits, guesses at a part, and then spends the evening writing up the visit. Ana did not brainstorm this problem. She managed it.

Tomás is a machine learning engineer who spent four years building speech and search systems. He met Ana through a mutual friend and spent two Saturdays riding along with her former colleagues. He came back with pages of notes and a strong view: technicians have their hands full and their eyes on the equipment, so a screen-first app is the wrong interface.

Here is how their idea holds up against this lesson:

- Make something people want: technicians already improvise solutions, such as calling colleagues, searching PDFs on their phones and keeping private notebooks of fixes. That is a sign of real demand.
- Tarpit check: "an AI assistant for workers" would be a tarpit. "A voice assistant that helps HVAC technicians diagnose equipment, find parts and file reports" names a user, a job and a buyer, the contractor.
- Founder–market fit: Ana brings insight and access. She can call twenty contractors this month. Tomás brings the technical depth to make voice work in noisy places.
- Why now: speech models have recently become accurate enough for job sites, language models can read long equipment manuals, and every technician already carries a smartphone.

They also wrote down what they do not know yet. Will contractors pay for this, or only technicians want it? Is diagnosis or paperwork the bigger pain? Those questions become next week's work.

## Common mistakes

- Starting from the technology ("we have a great model, what can it do?") instead of a specific person with a specific problem.
- Treating polite interest from friends and family as demand.
- Picking an idea because it sounds big, while ignoring that you have no access to its customers.
- A vague "why now" that would have been equally true three years ago.
- Falling in love with the first idea and refusing to look at evidence against it.

## Your turn

Take 20 minutes. Write your idea in one sentence using this form: "We help [specific user] do [specific job] by [approach], which matters now because [specific change]." Then answer the four test questions from the "Make something people want" section, and score yourself from 1 to 5 on insight, access and staying power. Write one action that would raise your lowest score.

## Key takeaways

- Demand shows up as behavior, especially existing workarounds and urgency, not compliments.
- Tarpit ideas are attractive, often tried and structurally hard. Ask what has changed before you commit.
- The best ideas are usually noticed by people close to a problem, not invented in a brainstorm.
- Founder–market fit means insight, access and staying power. You can earn it, but you cannot skip it.
- A good "why now" names a specific change and links it to a specific user.
