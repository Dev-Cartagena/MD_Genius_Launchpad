## Learning objectives

- Map every alternative your customer has today, including doing nothing, and explain why each one is chosen.
- Test whether your product is plausibly 10 times better on the dimension your customer cares about most.
- Articulate your company's secret, in Peter Thiel's sense, in one or two sentences.
- Write a positioning statement using Geoffrey Moore's template and check it against April Dunford's components.

## Overview

"We have no competitors" is one of the fastest ways to lose an investor's trust. Every customer with a real problem is already doing something about it, even if that something is a spreadsheet, a phone call or putting up with the pain. Your real competition is whatever they do today. AI makes this harder, not easier: incumbents can add AI features quickly, and foundation models give every competitor similar raw capability. Positioning is how you explain, in a few words, why a specific customer should choose you over everything else they could do. It shapes your pitch, your landing page and the questions in your next sales call.

## Your real competition

### Four kinds of alternative

When you list competitors, include all of these:

- Direct competitors: products built for the same job and the same customer.
- Indirect competitors: products built for something else that customers stretch to cover this job, such as a general tool or an existing suite with a new feature.
- In-house and manual workarounds: spreadsheets, group chats, a senior employee everyone calls, a binder of notes.
- Doing nothing: accepting the cost. This is often the strongest competitor, because it is free and requires no change.

Your interviews from week 3 are the best source. List what each person actually did the last time the problem came up. Count how often each alternative appeared. The most common answer is the one you have to beat first.

### Why customers pick each alternative

For each alternative, write down why customers choose it. The answer is rarely "they have not heard of anything better". It is usually that the alternative is already paid for, already trusted, or easy. Your product has to overcome that inertia, and the switching cost includes training, data migration and the risk of looking foolish if the new tool fails.

### The incumbent question for AI startups

Investors will ask what happens when the largest company in your space adds a similar AI feature. Answer it directly. Strong answers usually rest on the moats you studied in week 1: proprietary data that improves your product with use, deep embedding in a specific workflow, or distribution the incumbent cannot easily copy. A generic assistant built on the same model everyone else can call is not a moat.

## 10x better and the secret

### Why 10x

Peter Thiel argues in "Zero to One", the book that grew out of his Stanford startup course and the notes Blake Masters published from it, that a new technology should be at least 10 times better than its closest substitute on some important dimension. A small improvement does not overcome the switching cost and the risk of a new vendor. A large one makes the choice obvious.

The dimension matters more than the multiplier. Pick the one your customers care about most, based on your interviews: time, cost, accuracy, risk or effort. Then estimate honestly how much better you can be. If you cannot see a path to a dramatic improvement on something customers value, you are building a feature, not a company.

### Competition is for losers

In his lecture "Competition is for Losers", given in Sam Altman's 2014 Stanford course "How to Start a Startup", Thiel argues that companies in intense competition compete away their profits, while companies that create something new can dominate their category for a long time. His advice to founders: start with a small market you can own, then expand. This connects directly to last lesson's beachhead.

### What is your secret

Thiel asks founders to name a secret: an important truth that few people agree with you on. Good secrets usually come from close contact with a problem. They sound like "most of the value in this industry is lost at a step everyone ignores" or "the people who buy this are not the people the incumbents sell to". A secret explains why the opportunity exists and why others have missed it. Your week 3 interviews are where your secret should come from.

## Positioning

### Moore's positioning statement

Geoffrey Moore, in "Crossing the Chasm", offers a simple template that many founders use as a first draft:

- For: the target customer.
- Who: their compelling need or problem.
- Our product is a: the product category.
- That: the key benefit, the reason to buy.
- Unlike: the primary competitive alternative.
- Our product: the main difference that matters.

The value of the template is that it forces choices. You must name one customer, one alternative and one difference. If your statement still reads well with a competitor's name in it, it is not specific enough.

### Dunford's components

April Dunford, in "Obviously Awesome", treats positioning as context-setting and breaks it into five components that you work out in order:

1. Competitive alternatives: what customers would do if you did not exist.
2. Unique attributes: capabilities you have that the alternatives lack.
3. Value: what those attributes enable for customers, with proof.
4. Best-fit customers: the characteristics of those who care most about that value.
5. Market category: the frame that makes your value obvious.

Dunford's order is useful because it starts from the alternatives, not from your features, and ends with the category. Choosing the category is a strategic decision: "AI parts identification for field technicians" invites a different comparison from "field-service software".

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana and Tomás listed what each of their 18 interviewees did the last time a technician could not identify a part or a fault on site. These counts are the founders' own interview data.

- Called or messaged a senior technician, often after hours: the most common answer by far.
- Searched PDF or paper manufacturer manuals, often on a phone on a rooftop.
- Called the supply house or the manufacturer's support line and waited.
- Looked for a video or a forum post.
- Did nothing: came back another day.

Field-service-management suites appeared in every mid-size shop, but for scheduling, dispatch and job reports, not for diagnosis. Some vendors had started adding AI features. The founders noted this as their main incumbent risk.

Their 10x dimension was time to the right answer on site. Technicians described waits of many minutes to hours for a reply from a senior colleague. Fieldnote's target, still to be tested, is an answer in under two minutes, from the manuals and from the shop's own job history.

Their secret, drawn from the interviews: the knowledge that fixes most hard jobs on the first visit already exists, but it is trapped in a few senior technicians' heads and in manuals no one can search on a roof. As senior technicians retire, shops lose it.

Their first positioning statement:

For service managers at HVAC contractors with 20 to 200 technicians, who lose money and customers to repeat visits, Fieldnote is a voice-first AI assistant for technicians that identifies faults and parts on site in under two minutes. Unlike calling a senior technician or searching manuals, Fieldnote answers instantly, from the manufacturer's documentation and the shop's own job history, and it gets better with every job.

They noted that the last clause, the data flywheel, is their moat argument against incumbents, and that week 5 has to make it real.

## Common mistakes

- Claiming to have no competitors, which tells investors you have not looked.
- Leaving out doing nothing and manual workarounds, the alternatives most customers actually use.
- Claiming 10x on a dimension customers do not care about.
- Writing positioning that would still be true with a competitor's name in it.
- Answering the incumbent question with "we will move faster" and nothing else.

## Key takeaways

- Your real competition is what customers do today, including doing nothing.
- A new product must be dramatically better on the dimension customers value most to overcome switching costs.
- A secret explains why the opportunity exists and why others have missed it.
- Positioning names one customer, one alternative and one difference.
- AI startups must answer the incumbent question with a moat, not with speed alone.
