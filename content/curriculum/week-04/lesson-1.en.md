## Learning objectives

- Distinguish TAM, SAM and SOM, and explain why investors trust bottom-up estimates over top-down ones.
- Build a bottom-up market size from a count of customers and a price, with every input sourced or labeled as an estimate.
- Choose a beachhead market that you can realistically dominate first.
- Present a market size in a way that survives a skeptical investor's questions.

## Overview

Every seed investor asks how big this can get. Most founders answer with a large number from an industry report, and that is where the investor stops listening. A bottom-up figure, built from customers you can name and a price you have tested, shows that you understand how your business will make money. You now know who your customer is. This lesson turns that knowledge into a market size that is honest and defensible.

## TAM, SAM and SOM

### Three nested circles

- Total addressable market (TAM): the annual revenue available if every customer who could use your product bought it at your price.
- Serviceable addressable market (SAM): the part of the TAM you can reach with your current product, channels and geography.
- Serviceable obtainable market (SOM): the share of the SAM you can realistically win in the next few years, given your team, competition and sales capacity.

Write each one as revenue per year, not as a number of people or a single lifetime value. That makes them comparable with each other and with the revenue plan in your deck.

### Top-down versus bottom-up

Top-down starts with a big industry figure and takes a percentage. "The field-service software market is worth billions, and if we capture 1 percent..." The problem is that the 1 percent is a guess, the report's definition rarely matches your product, and nothing in the logic shows how you will win a single customer.

Bottom-up starts with the unit of purchase. Count the customers who match your ICP, multiply by the number of units each one buys, and multiply by the price per unit. Each input is something you can check, argue about and improve. When an investor challenges a number, you can show where it came from.

Use top-down only as a sanity check. If your bottom-up SAM is larger than the whole industry's reported spend, one of your inputs is wrong.

## Building a bottom-up estimate

### The formula

For most business products the core formula is simple:

1. Number of target customers that fit your ICP.
2. Times units per customer, such as seats, sites, vehicles or jobs per year.
3. Times annual price per unit.

The quality is in the inputs. Next to each one, write its source: a government statistic, a directory, a licensing database, a hand-counted sample or your interviews. If it is a guess, label it and say how you will test it. Investors respect a labeled assumption more than a confident number with no origin.

### Where to find the counts

Public sources are often better than paid reports: government labor and business statistics, association member lists, licensing registers and LinkedIn searches by job title and company size. These figures change every year, so note the date of each source.

When no source exists, sample. Count the matching companies in one city or state by hand, then scale with a stated method. Show the sample. A careful sample of one region is more credible than an unexplained national figure.

### Price comes from evidence

Use a price you have some reason to believe: what customers spend on their current workaround, what comparable tools charge, or what interviewees said about budget. It will change, and week 6 is about pricing. For now, make it explicit so others can see how sensitive the result is to it.

### Markets can grow

Bill Gurley's 2014 essay "How to Miss By a Mile" argued that a top-down estimate of Uber's market, based on the existing taxi market, missed the point: a much better product can expand the market by creating new use. AI products can do the same, for example by making a task cheap enough that people do it far more often. Treat this as an upside argument with its own evidence, not as a reason to inflate today's numbers.

## The beachhead market

### Win one place first

Geoffrey Moore, in "Crossing the Chasm", uses the D-Day landing as an analogy: take one beach, secure it, then use it as a base to move inland. Bill Aulet, who teaches entrepreneurship at MIT, makes choosing a beachhead one of the first steps of his "Disciplined Entrepreneurship" framework, right after segmenting the market. The idea in both is to choose a market narrow enough that you can become the obvious leader in it.

A good beachhead has:

- Customers with a sharp, shared pain and money to spend.
- A way to reach them that you can afford, such as an association, a community or a dense geography.
- Customers who talk to each other, so references travel.
- A product you can deliver now, without a long list of custom features.
- A clear next market that the beachhead helps you win.

### Small is fine if it leads somewhere

Peter Thiel argues in "Zero to One" that great companies start by dominating a small market and then expand. A small beachhead is not a weakness if you show the path it opens. Investors want to see that you can win the first market quickly and that the full TAM can support a venture-scale company.

## Presenting the market size

Put the logic on one slide or one sheet: the formula, each input, its source and the result for SOM, SAM and TAM. Add a sensitivity line: what happens if the price is 30 percent lower or there are half as many customers. A skeptical investor will try to break your number. Show that it holds with conservative inputs.

Three questions you should be ready to answer:

1. Who exactly are these customers, and how did you count them?
2. Why this price?
3. Why do you start here, and where do you go next?

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana and Tomás built their sheet from last week's ICP: HVAC contractors with 20 to 200 technicians. Every number is a founders' working estimate, to be refined as they go.

To count contractors, they sampled. They counted matching contractors in two states using association member lists and state licensing records, then scaled to the US using each state's share of HVAC employment in public labor statistics. Their working estimate is about 4,000 contractors in the beachhead, with an average of 50 technicians each, or about 200,000 technicians.

For price, they used a placeholder of 60 US dollars per technician per month, or 720 dollars per year. They chose it because managers described repeat visits costing far more than that per technician, and because it sits inside what shops already pay per user for software. Week 6 will test it.

- SAM, the beachhead: 200,000 technicians times 720 dollars is about 144 million dollars a year.
- TAM: the founders estimate about 1.2 million technicians across HVAC, electrical and solar service in the US at shops of every size. At the same price, that is about 864 million dollars a year, before expansion to other countries or trades.
- SOM: winning 3 percent of beachhead contractors within three years, or 120 shops of about 50 technicians, gives 6,000 technicians and about 4.3 million dollars of annual recurring revenue.

Their sensitivity line showed that even at 40 dollars per technician and half the contractor count, the beachhead was still worth about 48 million dollars a year. The beachhead also passed the tests above: shared pain, a reachable association channel, managers who know each other, and a next step into electrical and solar service, where the same diagnosis and parts problem exists.

## Common mistakes

- Leading with a top-down industry figure and a "we only need 1 percent" argument.
- Mixing units, such as counting companies but pricing per user.
- Giving inputs without sources or dates.
- Choosing a beachhead so broad that you cannot reach or dominate it.
- Inflating today's market with speculative expansion instead of presenting expansion as a separate upside.

## Your turn

Build a first bottom-up sheet. Write your ICP in one line, then the three inputs, customer count, units per customer and price, each with a source or a "guess" label. Calculate SAM for your beachhead and a rough TAM. Add one sensitivity line. Spend 25 to 30 minutes, then ask your co-founder to attack the weakest input.

## Key takeaways

- Investors trust market sizes they can rebuild from named customers, units and a price.
- Every input needs a source and a date, or a clear label as an estimate.
- Top-down figures are a sanity check, not an argument.
- A beachhead is the market you can dominate first, chosen for sharp pain and reachable, connected customers.
- Show the path from the beachhead to a market large enough for a venture-scale company.
