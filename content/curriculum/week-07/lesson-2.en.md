## Learning objectives

- Build a founder-led sales pipeline with defined stages, exit criteria and a weekly review.
- Work backward from a customer goal to the number of conversations you need each week.
- Run a discovery call that uncovers pain, impact, the decision process and a concrete next step.
- Design a paid pilot with written success criteria that converts into a contract, and turn early customers into references.

## Overview

Your first ten paying customers matter more than any other ten you will ever sign. They prove that someone will pay, they teach you how buyers decide, and they become the references that make customers eleven to one hundred easier. They almost never arrive on their own. YC's Startup School talks on sales, including Max Kolysh's "How to Get Your First 10 Customers" and Tom Blomfield's "The Sales Playbook for Founders", make the same point from different angles: early customers come from founders doing direct, manual, persistent work. This lesson turns that work into a repeatable process.

## Build a pipeline you can run

A pipeline is a list of real companies, each in a defined stage, with a next step and a date. Without one, founders confuse activity with progress.

### Start with a target list

Write down 50 to 100 named companies that match your ideal customer profile from week 3. For each, find the person who feels the pain and the person who signs. Start with warm paths: former colleagues, investors, advisors, customers' peers, industry groups. Cold outreach works too, but it should be short, specific to the recipient's problem and written by a founder.

### Define stages with exit criteria

Each stage needs a rule for when a deal moves forward, so you cannot fool yourself. A simple version:

1. Target: on the list, not yet contacted.
2. Contacted: a founder has reached out at least twice.
3. Discovery: a call took place and the pain was confirmed.
4. Pilot proposed: written scope, success criteria and price sent.
5. Pilot live: signed, the customer is using the product.
6. Closed: a paid contract is signed, or the deal is marked lost with a reason.

Keep this in a spreadsheet or a simple CRM. Review it every week as a team: what moved, what stalled and why.

### Work backward from the goal

Pick a goal and divide by your conversion rates. If you want 10 customers and your own early data says half of pilots convert, you need 20 pilots. If one in three discovery calls becomes a pilot, you need about 60 calls. If one in five contacts takes a call, you need about 300 contacts. Spread across ten weeks, that is 30 contacts and 6 calls a week. Use your real rates as soon as you have them. The numbers usually show founders that they are doing a fraction of the outreach their goal requires.

## Run discovery calls that find the truth

Discovery is not a demo. Its job is to learn whether this prospect has a problem worth solving, whether they can buy and what would make them buy now. Use the Mom Test habits from week 3: ask about past behavior, not opinions about the future.

### A simple call structure

1. Agenda, two minutes: say what you will cover and ask what they want to get out of it.
2. Current process: how do they handle the problem today, who is involved and what tools they use.
3. Impact: what the problem costs in time, money, risk or lost customers. Ask for numbers.
4. Decision process: who else cares, who signs, what budget it comes from and what a purchase usually involves.
5. Next step: a dated, specific commitment, such as a second call with the owner or a pilot scope review.

Talk less than half the time. Take notes in the customer's own words, because those phrases become your website copy and deck.

### Champions and economic buyers

In most B2B deals two roles matter. The champion feels the pain, wants your product and will push it internally. The economic buyer controls the budget and says yes. Most B2B sales methods stress finding both early. A champion with no access to the buyer leads to enthusiastic pilots that never close. Ask your champion directly: "What would it take for this to be approved, and can we meet that person together?"

## Pilots that turn into contracts

AI products often need a pilot, because buyers want proof on their own data. Pilots are also where many startups stall, running unpaid trials that never end.

### Design the pilot before it starts

Agree on these points in writing before the pilot begins:

- Scope: which users, which sites, which workflows.
- Duration: short and fixed, often four to eight weeks.
- Success criteria: two or three measurable outcomes the buyer cares about, with a baseline.
- Price: ask for payment where you can. Even a discounted fee filters out tourists and gives the pilot a budget owner.
- Conversion: what happens if the criteria are met, including price, term and who signs.

A pilot without success criteria is a free trial. A pilot without a conversion clause is a negotiation you will have to start over.

### Run it like a project

Assign a founder to each pilot. Hold a short weekly check-in with the champion, share progress against the success criteria, and fix problems in days, not weeks. Paul Graham's advice in "Do Things That Don't Scale" applies here: onboarding customers by hand teaches you what to build next.

### Closing

Closing is mostly asking. Two weeks before the pilot ends, review results with the champion and the economic buyer and propose the contract. Handle objections directly. Price objections often hide value doubts, so return to the success criteria. If a deal is lost, write down why. Lost deals are some of your best product data.

### References

After a customer is live and happy, ask for three things: a reference call for prospects, a short quote you can use and an introduction to a peer. Make each one easy by drafting it for them. In a niche market, each reference makes the next sale faster.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Fieldnote's goal for the quarter is 10 paying contractors. They start with three pilots from week 6. Ana proposed conversion terms to all three: a 12-month contract priced per technician, effective when the pilot met two criteria agreed at the start, a measurable improvement in first-time-fix rate and technician weekly usage above a set threshold. Two pilots converted. The third did not, because the owner never met the founders and did not trust the numbers his operations manager showed him. The founders' lesson: meet the economic buyer before the pilot starts, not after.

Ana built a target list of 80 contractors with 20 to 200 technicians in their region, using the membership directory of a regional HVAC contractors association, supplier introductions and referrals from pilot customers. Their stages match the six above. Using their own early rates, roughly one in four warm contacts accepting a call, one in three calls leading to a pilot and about two in three pilots converting, they need 8 more customers, so about 12 more pilots, about 36 discovery calls and about 140 contacts. Over ten weeks, that is 14 contacts and 4 calls a week, which Ana can handle while Tomás builds.

Their discovery calls now open with one question: "Walk me through the last callback your team had to make." Operations managers answer with a story, a cost and often a name. Every converted customer gets asked for a reference call and an introduction to one peer contractor.

## Common mistakes

- Running demos instead of discovery, so you never learn how the customer buys.
- Starting unpaid pilots with no success criteria or conversion terms.
- Selling only to the champion and meeting the economic buyer at the end, or never.
- Doing a fraction of the outreach the goal requires and blaming the market.
- Hiring a salesperson to fix a process the founders have not yet made work.

## Your turn

Take 25 minutes. Write your goal for paying customers this quarter. Using your real conversion rates, or honest estimates labeled as guesses, work backward to weekly contacts and calls. Then draft a one-page pilot proposal for your best prospect with scope, duration, two or three success criteria with baselines, price and conversion terms.

## Key takeaways

- The first ten customers come from founders doing direct, manual, persistent work.
- A pipeline with stages and exit criteria shows real progress. Review it every week.
- Work backward from your goal to weekly outreach. The numbers are usually higher than founders expect.
- Discovery is about pain, impact and the decision process, and it always ends with a dated next step.
- Pilots need success criteria, a price and conversion terms agreed before they start.
