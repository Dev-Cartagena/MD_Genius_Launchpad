## Learning objectives

- Compare founder-led sales, product-led growth and enterprise sales, and explain what each one demands of your product, price and team.
- Choose a go-to-market motion for your startup using four inputs: who buys, what it costs, how long it takes to buy and how fast users see value.
- Place your beachhead on the technology adoption life cycle and describe the "chasm" your company has to cross.
- Define the whole product your first mainstream customers will need before they can buy.

## Overview

By week 7 you have evidence that someone wants what you are building. The next question is how that someone finds you, decides to buy and keeps paying. That system is your go-to-market (GTM) motion. It shapes who you hire, how you price and how much you need to raise. AI products can be demoed to anyone in minutes, which tempts founders to sell to everyone at once. The fastest-growing companies usually pick one motion, one buyer and one segment, and master it before adding a second.

## The three motions

There are many hybrids, but almost every early-stage company starts from one of three motions. Each is a bet about where the buying decision happens.

### Founder-led sales

The founders themselves find prospects, run discovery calls, set up pilots and close deals. Nearly every B2B startup should begin here, even ones that later become product-led. YC's Startup School talks on sales make founder-led selling a core theme, for a simple reason: nobody else can sell an unfinished product with the same conviction, or will hear the objections that should change the roadmap. Paul Graham's essay "Do Things That Don't Scale" argues that recruiting users by hand is how most successful startups got going.

It fits when deals are worth a few thousand dollars a year or more, when the buyer is a manager rather than an individual user, and when you still need to learn what the customer actually values.

### Product-led growth

In product-led growth (PLG), the product does most of the selling. Users sign up alone, get value quickly, pay with a card and spread the product inside their organization. Slack, Figma and many AI coding tools grew this way. PLG requires three things that are hard to fake: a user who can start without permission from anyone, a time-to-value of minutes rather than weeks, and a price low enough to put on a card or a team budget.

Be careful: a free tier with high inference costs can burn cash quickly. Before you go product-led, check your gross margin per active free user, a number you worked out in week 6.

### Enterprise sales

Enterprise sales targets large organizations with long cycles, many stakeholders, security reviews and procurement. Contracts are large and the motion needs account executives, sales engineers and customer success. Few seed-stage startups should run a pure enterprise motion. Many should sell founder-led to one plant, region or department of a large company and grow from there. Pete Koomen's Startup School talk "Enterprise Sales for Founders" walks through how a technical founder can run that funnel personally before hiring sales staff.

## How to choose

Use four inputs. Write your honest answer to each before you pick.

1. Who makes the buying decision? If it is the user, PLG is possible. If it is a manager with a budget, you need sales. If it is a committee with procurement, you need enterprise sales, or you need a smaller first buyer.
2. What is the likely annual contract value? As a rough rule of thumb, small contracts cannot pay for a salesperson's time and big contracts cannot close without one. The exact thresholds vary by market, so test yours.
3. How long does it take to buy? Count the steps between "interested" and "signed": approvals, a security review, a pilot, legal. Each step adds weeks.
4. How fast does a user see value? If value shows up in the first session, self-serve works. If it shows up after an integration or a workflow change, someone has to guide the customer.

When the answers point in different directions, choose the motion that matches the buyer, not the user. Enthusiastic users who cannot sign a contract are a channel into the account, which lesson 3 covers, not a sales motion.

### Motions change over time

Your first motion is not your last. A common path is founder-led sales to the first ten or twenty customers, then a playbook a first sales hire can run, then a self-serve tier or an enterprise team. Write down the signal that would tell you to change, such as "half of new sign-ups arrive without us contacting them".

## Crossing the chasm

Geoffrey Moore's book "Crossing the Chasm" builds on the technology adoption life cycle, which splits a market into innovators, early adopters, the early majority, the late majority and laggards. Moore's insight is that the gap between early adopters and the early majority is not a small step. It is a chasm.

### Why the chasm exists

Early adopters buy a vision. They tolerate gaps and want an edge over peers. The early majority are pragmatists. They buy a proven solution to a known problem, they want references from companies like theirs, and they hate risk. What wins early adopters, such as novelty and a bold roadmap, does not win pragmatists. Many startups stall here with happy early adopters and a next wave that will not move.

### The beachhead strategy

Moore's answer is to concentrate on one narrow niche, which he compares to a beachhead, and to win it completely. Owning one segment gives pragmatists references, because buyers in a niche talk to each other. Once you own the first niche, you move to adjacent ones that share customers, channels or use cases. Moore calls this the bowling-pin approach: each pin you knock down helps knock down the next.

### The whole product

Pragmatists do not buy software. They buy an outcome. The whole product is everything they need to get that outcome: the core product plus onboarding, integrations, training, support, documentation and sometimes partners. For an AI product the whole product usually also includes an explanation of how it handles errors, who reviews low-confidence outputs, what happens to customer data and how the customer can measure results. List these pieces. The gap between what you ship and what the buyer needs often blocks the deal.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Fieldnote has three pilot contractors and a product-market-fit survey in which 46% of technicians said they would be very disappointed without it (founders' own data from week 6). Ana and Tomás now need a GTM motion.

They answered the four questions. The technician is the user, but the buyer is the operations manager or service manager at a mid-size HVAC contractor with 20 to 200 technicians, and the owner signs anything large. Contract value depends on fleet size, because they price per technician. Buying takes a pilot plus the owner's approval, roughly four to eight weeks in their three pilots. Technicians see value on the first job, but managers only see value after a few weeks of first-time-fix data.

That pointed clearly to founder-led sales to operations managers, with Ana leading. As a former HVAC operations lead, she speaks the buyer's language about callbacks, truck rolls and the busy season. Tomás joins technical calls.

They rejected pure PLG. Technicians love the product, but they do not own a budget, and a free tier would carry real inference costs per voice query. They also rejected large national service companies for now. They would need security reviews, procurement and integration with their field-service-management suite, a whole product Fieldnote cannot yet deliver.

Their beachhead is the one from week 3: mid-size HVAC contractors in one region. Their whole-product list includes a one-hour technician onboarding, parts lookup tied to the contractor's distributors, a weekly first-time-fix report for the manager and a one-page note on how voice data is stored and reviewed. Their signal for changing motion: when a contractor signs without a founder ever meeting them in person.

## Common mistakes

- Picking PLG because it sounds scalable when the user cannot buy and the value takes weeks to appear.
- Hiring a salesperson before the founders have closed deals themselves and written down how they did it.
- Selling to every segment that shows interest, so no segment ever has enough references to convince pragmatists.
- Treating the core AI model as the whole product and ignoring onboarding, integration, data handling and error review.

Then write your whole-product list: the core product plus every other piece a pragmatic buyer would need to say yes. Mark what you lack, and write one signal that would tell you to change motion.

## Key takeaways

- Your GTM motion is a bet about where the buying decision happens. Match it to the buyer, not the user.
- Almost every B2B startup should start with founder-led sales, even if it later becomes product-led.
- PLG needs self-serve users, value within minutes and a low price. AI inference costs make free tiers riskier.
- Early adopters buy a vision. Pragmatists buy a proven whole product and references from peers.
- Win one narrow beachhead completely before moving to the next segment.
