## Learning objectives

- Describe the five modes of the Stanford d.school design process and when founders use each.
- Run a short empathy session and turn what you observe into a point-of-view problem statement.
- Generate how-might-we questions that open up solutions without prescribing one.
- Build an assumption map and pick the riskiest assumptions to test first.

## Overview

Founders are trained to look for answers, but at this stage the bigger risk is answering the wrong question. Design thinking, as taught at the Stanford d.school, is a disciplined way to understand people before you decide what to build. It pairs well with the customer development work you will do next week. Design thinking helps you see the problem clearly. Customer development helps you test whether a business can be built around it. For AI startups, where a prototype is cheap and fast, this discipline keeps you from building an impressive solution to a problem nobody described.

## The d.school modes, starting with empathy

The Stanford d.school describes design work as a set of modes: empathize, define, ideate, prototype and test. They are called modes, not steps, because in practice you move back and forth between them. A test can send you back to empathize, and a new insight can send you back to define. This week focuses on the first three. Prototyping and testing return in weeks 3 to 5, when you design experiments and an MVP.

The d.school's free "Design Thinking Bootleg" collects dozens of methods for each mode. It is worth keeping on hand throughout the program.

### Observe, engage, immerse

Empathy here is not a feeling. It is a research practice. The goal is to understand what people do, think and feel in the situation you care about, including what they would never think to tell you. The d.school groups this into three activities:

- Observe: watch people do the work in their real setting. Note what they do, not only what they say.
- Engage: talk with them. Ask them to walk you through the last time something happened, and ask "why" more than feels natural.
- Immerse: try the experience yourself. Spend a day doing the job, using their tools, under their constraints.

### What to capture

During and after each session, write down:

- Quotes, word for word, especially surprising ones.
- Workarounds: notes taped to equipment, private spreadsheets, phone calls to a colleague.
- Contradictions: when what someone says differs from what they do. These are often the richest insights.
- Emotions: moments of frustration, embarrassment, pride or relief.

A simple tool is an empathy map with four areas: what the person says, does, thinks and feels. Fill it in right after each session while your memory is fresh.

## Define

### From observations to a point of view

The define mode turns raw observations into a clear, specific problem statement. The d.school calls this a point of view, built from three parts: a user, a need and an insight.

"[User] needs a way to [need] because [insight]."

The need should be a verb, describing what the person is trying to do, not a feature. The insight is the surprising thing you learned that makes the problem interesting. Compare these two:

- Weak: "Technicians need a better app because current apps are bad."
- Strong: "A newer HVAC technician on a solo job needs a way to confirm a diagnosis before ordering a part, because calling a senior colleague feels like admitting they are not ready to work alone."

The strong version names a specific user, an action and an insight with emotional weight. It also suggests directions to explore without dictating a solution.

### Checking your statement

A good point-of-view statement:

- Is specific enough that you could find ten such users this week.
- Describes a need as an action, not a product.
- Contains an insight you did not have before you went into the field.
- Could be solved in several different ways.

## Ideate

### How-might-we questions

How-might-we questions turn a problem statement into prompts for ideas. They are widely used at the d.school and at design firms such as IDEO. "How" assumes a solution exists, "might" says it may or may not work, and "we" makes it a shared effort.

From the point of view above, you might ask:

- How might we help a newer technician confirm a diagnosis without calling anyone?
- How might we make asking for help feel like a normal part of the job?
- How might we make the first visit enough, even on unfamiliar equipment?

Good how-might-we questions are neither too broad ("How might we fix field service?") nor too narrow ("How might we add a button that calls a colleague?").

### Generating and choosing ideas

Set a timer and generate many ideas per question, without judging them. Quantity matters at this stage, because the first ideas are usually the obvious ones. Then cluster similar ideas and choose a few to carry forward, using criteria you agree on in advance, such as most likely to delight users, most feasible this month and most different from what exists.

## Assumption mapping

### Every idea rests on assumptions

Each idea you carry forward rests on beliefs that might be false. David J. Bland, co-author with Alexander Osterwalder of "Testing Business Ideas", popularized assumption mapping as a fast way to find which beliefs to test first. Assumptions fall into three groups:

- Desirability: do the people we serve want this? Does the problem matter enough?
- Viability: can this be a business? Will someone pay, at a price that works?
- Feasibility: can we build and deliver it? For AI products, this includes model quality, data access and cost.

### Building the map

1. Write each assumption on its own card, as a statement that could turn out to be false: "Contractors will pay for fewer repeat visits."
2. Place each card on a two-by-two grid. One axis is importance: if this were false, would the idea fail? The other is evidence: do we have real evidence for this, or are we guessing?
3. Focus on the corner that is important and has little evidence. Those are your riskiest assumptions.
4. For each of the top three, write how you could test it cheaply in the next two weeks and what result would prove you wrong.

The map is a living document. Update it every week as evidence comes in.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana and Tomás ran six empathy sessions. They rode along on four service calls, interviewed two technicians at the end of their shifts and spent one afternoon trying to diagnose a unit themselves using only the manufacturer's PDF on a phone.

Three things stood out. A newer technician kept a private notebook of fixes and was embarrassed when they saw it. Two technicians said the paperwork was "the worst part", yet both described the last bad day in detail as a misdiagnosis, not a report. And Tomás, trying the PDF himself, gave up after twenty minutes of scrolling with gloves on.

Their point of view: "A newer technician working alone needs a way to reach a confident diagnosis on unfamiliar equipment, because a wrong guess means a second visit and calling for help feels like failing."

Their how-might-we questions included "How might we turn the senior technician's know-how into something available on every job?" and "How might we make documentation happen as a side effect of the work?"

Their assumption map, riskiest first:

- Paperwork is technicians' biggest pain and the main reason to buy. Important, and their evidence is mixed, since the stories point to diagnosis instead.
- Contractors, not technicians, will pay for the product. Important, with little evidence.
- Voice recognition works reliably in loud mechanical rooms. Important for feasibility, with only early prototype evidence.
- Technicians will trust AI suggestions enough to act on them. Important, with no evidence yet.

The top two go straight into next week's customer interviews.

## Common mistakes

- Treating empathy as a survey rather than watching people do the real work.
- Writing a problem statement that already contains your solution.
- How-might-we questions so broad they produce nothing useful, or so narrow they lead to one feature.
- Listing assumptions without ranking them, so you test the comfortable ones first.
- Treating the assumption map as finished after one session.

## Your turn

Take 30 minutes. Write one point-of-view statement using the user, need and insight format, based on something you have actually observed. Write three how-might-we questions from it. Then list eight assumptions behind your idea, tag each as desirability, viability or feasibility, and circle the two that are most important and least supported by evidence.

## Key takeaways

- The d.school modes help you understand people before you decide what to build.
- Empathy is a research practice: observe, engage and immerse, and capture contradictions.
- A strong point of view names a user, a need as an action and a real insight.
- How-might-we questions open up solutions without prescribing one.
- Assumption mapping shows which beliefs, if wrong, would sink you, so you test those first.
