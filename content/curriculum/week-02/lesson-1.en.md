## Learning objectives

- Score a problem on severity, frequency and willingness to pay, and compare problems with those scores.
- Apply the "hair on fire" test to tell urgent problems from nice-to-have ones.
- Describe the job your customer is hiring a product to do, using Jobs-to-be-Done.
- Separate what you know about a problem from what you are estimating, and label each.

## Overview

Last week you wrote down an idea. This week you look underneath it. Ideas change constantly in the first months of a startup, but a well-chosen problem stays steady while solutions come and go. Founders who define the problem precisely can change their product quickly without losing direction. Founders who skip this step tend to build features in search of a purpose. With AI, the temptation to skip is stronger than ever, because you can build something interesting before you know who needs it.

## Severity, frequency and willingness to pay

### Three questions that matter most

Not every real problem is worth a company. To be worth solving, a problem usually needs to score well on three dimensions:

- Severity: how much does it hurt when it happens? Measure it in time lost, money lost, risk taken or stress felt. A problem that costs a business a customer is more severe than one that costs an employee five minutes.
- Frequency: how often does it happen? A painful problem that happens once a year is hard to build a habit around. A moderate problem that happens every day can support a product people use constantly.
- Willingness to pay: does someone with a budget care enough to pay to make it go away? The person who feels the pain is not always the person who pays. In business software, a user may suffer while a manager signs the check.

### Scoring and comparing

Score each dimension from 1 to 5 for every problem you are considering. Write down the evidence next to each score. A 5 for severity backed by "a customer told me they lost a contract over this" means much more than a 5 backed by "it seems annoying". The point is not the math. The point is that writing evidence beside each number shows quickly where you are guessing.

The best problems are severe and frequent, and they belong to a buyer who already spends money on them. If you find a problem people already pay to solve badly, through consultants, extra staff or clumsy software, you have found a budget to win.

## The "hair on fire" test

### Urgent versus nice to have

YC partners often describe the ideal early customer as someone whose hair is on fire. If your hair is on fire, you will accept a bucket of water from a stranger. You do not ask about the brand of the bucket or whether it comes in other colors. You want the fire out now.

Customers with a hair-on-fire problem behave in recognizable ways:

- They have already tried to solve it, often with a patchwork of tools or manual effort.
- They can describe the last time it happened in detail, without prompting.
- They will make time to talk to you, and they ask when they can try your solution.
- They will accept an unpolished product, because the problem costs them more than the rough edges do.

A nice-to-have problem looks different. People agree it is a problem, say they would "probably" use a solution and then never follow up. That is not rejection, but it is not demand either.

### Using the test early

You do not need a product to apply the test. In each conversation, ask what the person did the last time the problem happened, what it cost them and what they have tried. If nobody has tried anything, the problem is probably not urgent. Next week you will learn interview methods that make these answers more reliable.

### Look for the unglamorous

Paul Graham's essay "Schlep Blindness" points out that founders unconsciously avoid problems that involve tedious, unpleasant work, such as dealing with regulations, paperwork or messy industries. That avoidance leaves valuable problems unsolved. Many of the best AI opportunities sit inside exactly these schleps: field operations, compliance, billing and logistics.

## Jobs-to-be-Done

### Customers hire products

Jobs-to-be-Done is a way of thinking developed and popularized by Harvard professor Clayton Christensen and his colleagues. The core idea is that customers do not buy products. They "hire" them to make progress in a particular situation. When a product does the job poorly, they "fire" it and hire something else.

The Christensen Institute's well-known example is a fast-food chain trying to sell more milkshakes. Asking customers what they wanted in a milkshake led nowhere. Observing when and why people bought them did. Many were commuters buying in the morning, who wanted something that lasted through a long, boring drive and kept them full until lunch. Their real competition was not other milkshakes. It was bananas, bagels and boredom.

### Writing a job statement

A good job statement describes the situation, the motivation and the outcome, with no mention of your product:

"When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [expected outcome]."

Jobs have three dimensions, and the strongest products serve all of them:

- Functional: the practical task, such as finding the right part.
- Emotional: how the person wants to feel, such as confident and not embarrassed in front of a customer.
- Social: how they want to be seen, such as a technician who fixes it on the first visit.

### Why it helps AI founders

AI founders often describe their product as a capability ("we summarize documents"). A job statement forces you to describe the progress the customer wants. It also reveals the real competition: the colleague they phone, the spreadsheet they keep or the decision to do nothing at all.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana and Tomás wrote down three problems their assistant could address and scored each one.

- Diagnosis on site: severity 5, because a wrong diagnosis means a second visit and an unhappy customer. Frequency 4, because it happens on many jobs. Willingness to pay 4, since contractors already pay for senior technicians' time on the phone. Evidence: Ana's years of managing it, plus five technician conversations.
- Paperwork after the visit: severity 3, frequency 5, willingness to pay 3. Evidence: technicians complain about it constantly, but no contractor Ana knows pays to fix it.
- Parts lookup: severity 4, frequency 4, willingness to pay 4. Evidence: wrong parts cause repeat truck rolls, which contractors track closely.

Based on Ana's experience, the founders estimate that technicians lose about 30 to 40 percent of a visit to diagnosis and paperwork. They labeled this clearly as their own estimate, to be validated, not as a fact. They noticed that, without meaning to, they had been pitching Fieldnote mostly as a paperwork tool, because paperwork is what technicians complain about most.

Their job statement for the technician:

"When I am at a unit I do not recognize and the customer is watching, I want to find the likely cause and the right part quickly, so I can fix it on this visit and move on to my next job."

For the buyer, the contractor's operations manager, the job is different: "When I schedule my crews, I want fewer repeat visits, so I can serve more customers with the technicians I have."

The hair-on-fire test gave them a warning. Technicians complain loudly about paperwork, but complaining is not paying. The urgent, budgeted pain might be diagnosis and parts. They decided not to settle this by debate and to test it in the field.

## Common mistakes

- Scoring problems without writing the evidence next to each score.
- Confusing the loudest complaint with the most valuable problem.
- Assuming the user and the buyer feel the same pain.
- Writing a job statement that describes your product instead of the customer's progress.
- Presenting your own estimates as facts in a pitch or memo.

## Your turn

Take 25 minutes. List up to three problems your idea could address. Score each on severity, frequency and willingness to pay from 1 to 5, with one line of evidence per score. Mark each piece of evidence as "observed", "heard" or "guessed". Then write one job statement for your user and one for your buyer, if they are different people.

## Key takeaways

- A problem worth solving is severe, frequent and owned by someone with a budget.
- Hair-on-fire customers have already tried to fix the problem and will accept a rough solution.
- Customers hire products to make progress in a specific situation; describe that job, not your product.
- Your real competition is often a workaround or doing nothing.
- Label estimates as estimates until you have evidence.
