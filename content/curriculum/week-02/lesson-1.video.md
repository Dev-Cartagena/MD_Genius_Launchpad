# Video: Finding a problem worth solving
Duration: ~3:45 · Format: 16:9 1920×1080 · Presenter: Maya Ortiz — calm, mid-30s Latin American founder-turned-investor, navy blazer, neutral studio

## Scene 1 — Hook (0:00–0:20)
- Type: presenter
- On screen: Maya centered. Lower third: "Week 2 · Finding a problem worth solving". Behind the title, the word "Solution" in Steel #3A607D slides aside to reveal "Problem" in White Smoke.
- Narration EN: In the first months of a startup, your solution will change many times. A well-chosen problem will not. If you define the problem precisely, you can change the product quickly without losing your way. Today we learn how to pick a problem worth a company.
- Narración ES: En los primeros meses de una startup, tu solución va a cambiar muchas veces. Un problema bien elegido, no. Si defines el problema con precisión, puedes cambiar el producto rápido sin perder el rumbo. Hoy aprenderemos a elegir un problema que justifique una empresa.

## Scene 2 — Three dimensions (0:20–0:50)
- Type: motion
- On screen: Three vertical gauges rise in Mist #97B7C1 on Navy #082B42: "Severity", "Frequency", "Willingness to pay". Each fills to a different level. Beside each gauge a small evidence tag appears: "observed", "heard", "guessed", the last one in a dashed outline. A caption appears: "Write the evidence next to every score."
- Narration EN: Score every problem on three dimensions. Severity: how much it hurts in time, money or risk. Frequency: how often it happens. And willingness to pay: does someone with a budget care enough to pay to make it go away? Remember that the user who feels the pain is not always the buyer. Write your evidence next to each score, and mark whether you observed it, heard it or guessed it.
- Narración ES: Califica cada problema en tres dimensiones. Gravedad: cuánto duele en tiempo, dinero o riesgo. Frecuencia: con qué frecuencia ocurre. Y disposición a pagar: ¿a alguien con presupuesto le importa lo suficiente como para pagar por resolverlo? Recuerda que el usuario que siente el dolor no siempre es el comprador. Anota tu evidencia junto a cada calificación e indica si la observaste, la escuchaste o la supusiste.

## Scene 3 — Hair on fire (0:50–1:20)
- Type: split
- On screen: Left, Maya. Right, two columns. "Hair on fire" in White Smoke with four ticks: "Already tried to fix it", "Recalls the last time in detail", "Makes time to talk", "Accepts a rough product". "Nice to have" in muted Steel with one line: "Agrees, says probably, never follows up."
- Narration EN: YC partners describe the ideal early customer as someone whose hair is on fire. They will take a bucket of water from a stranger and not ask about the brand. You can spot them. They have already tried to fix the problem, they remember the last time it happened, they make time to talk and they accept a rough product. A nice-to-have problem sounds friendly, and then nobody follows up.
- Narración ES: Los socios de YC describen al cliente inicial ideal como alguien con el pelo en llamas. Acepta un balde de agua de un desconocido y no pregunta por la marca. Puedes reconocerlo. Ya intentó resolver el problema, recuerda la última vez que ocurrió, hace tiempo para hablar y acepta un producto sin pulir. Un problema deseable suena amable, y después nadie vuelve a escribir.

## Scene 4 — Jobs-to-be-Done (1:20–1:55)
- Type: motion
- On screen: A milkshake cup icon in Mist with a car and a sunrise behind it. Labels orbit it: "long commute", "full until lunch", and the competitors "banana", "bagel", "boredom" appear and fade. The scene resolves into a template card: "When [situation], I want to [motivation], so I can [outcome]." Three small tags slide under it: "Functional", "Emotional", "Social".
- Narration EN: Clayton Christensen and his colleagues gave us Jobs-to-be-Done. Customers do not buy products. They hire them to make progress in a situation. In the Christensen Institute's milkshake example, morning commuters hired a milkshake to get through a long drive and stay full until lunch. Its real competitors were bananas, bagels and boredom. Write your customer's job as: when, I want to, so I can. And remember every job has a functional, an emotional and a social side.
- Narración ES: Clayton Christensen y sus colegas nos dieron Jobs-to-be-Done. Los clientes no compran productos. Los contratan para avanzar en una situación. En el ejemplo de las malteadas del Christensen Institute, quienes viajaban al trabajo por la mañana contrataban una malteada para aguantar un trayecto largo y llegar satisfechos al almuerzo. Su verdadera competencia eran los plátanos, los bagels y el aburrimiento. Escribe el trabajo de tu cliente así: cuando, quiero, para poder. Y recuerda que todo trabajo tiene un lado funcional, uno emocional y uno social.

## Scene 5 — Look for the schlep (1:55–2:15)
- Type: motion
- On screen: Stacked icons of a clipboard, a regulation document and a delivery truck in Steel. The caption "Schlep blindness · Paul Graham" fades in, then "Valuable problems hide in tedious work."
- Narration EN: Paul Graham calls it schlep blindness. Founders avoid tedious, messy problems, and that leaves valuable ones unsolved. Many of the best AI opportunities live in field work, compliance and billing.
- Narración ES: Paul Graham lo llama schlep blindness, la ceguera ante lo tedioso. Los fundadores evitan los problemas pesados y desordenados, y así quedan sin resolver algunos muy valiosos. Muchas de las mejores oportunidades de IA están en el trabajo de campo, el cumplimiento normativo y la facturación.

## Scene 6 — Fieldnote scores its problems (2:15–2:50)
- Type: broll
- On screen: Image with a slow pan. Overlay: a small scorecard in White Smoke with three rows, "Diagnosis 5·4·4", "Paperwork 3·5·3", "Parts 4·4·4", and a note "30–40% of a visit: founders' estimate, to be validated".
- Image prompt (if broll): Photorealistic documentary photo of a South Asian woman HVAC technician in her late twenties crouched in front of an open residential furnace in a basement, flashlight in one hand, looking closely at a wiring diagram on the inside of the panel door, a homeowner visible out of focus on the stairs, warm practical lighting, candid, no logos or readable text.
- Narration EN: Fieldnote scored three problems: diagnosis, paperwork and parts lookup. The founders estimate that technicians lose thirty to forty percent of a visit to diagnosis and paperwork. That is their estimate, and they label it that way. Their pitch had drifted toward paperwork, because that is what technicians complain about most. But complaining is not paying. The urgent, budgeted pain may be diagnosis and parts, so they will test it.
- Narración ES: Fieldnote calificó tres problemas: diagnóstico, papeleo y búsqueda de repuestos. Los fundadores estiman que los técnicos pierden entre treinta y cuarenta por ciento de una visita en diagnóstico y papeleo. Es su estimación, y así la etiquetan. Su pitch se había ido hacia el papeleo, porque es de lo que más se quejan los técnicos. Pero quejarse no es pagar. El dolor urgente y con presupuesto puede estar en el diagnóstico y los repuestos, así que lo van a poner a prueba.

## Scene 7 — Run the test in a conversation (2:50–3:30)
- Type: presenter
- On screen: Maya, medium close-up. Three questions appear at screen right in White Smoke, one by one: "What did you do the last time this happened?", "What did it cost you?", "What have you tried?". A final caption in Mist #97B7C1: "Nobody tried anything? Probably not urgent."
- Narration EN: You do not need a product to run the hair-on-fire test. In every conversation this week, ask three questions. What did you do the last time this happened? What did it cost you? And what have you already tried? Listen for specific stories, real numbers and existing workarounds. If nobody you talk to has tried anything, the problem is probably not urgent, however often they mention it. And be careful to separate the person who feels the pain from the person who pays. Next week you will learn interview methods that make these answers even more reliable.
- Narración ES: No necesitas un producto para hacer la prueba del pelo en llamas. En cada conversación de esta semana, haz tres preguntas. ¿Qué hiciste la última vez que pasó esto? ¿Cuánto te costó? ¿Y qué has intentado ya? Escucha historias concretas, números reales y soluciones improvisadas que ya existen. Si nadie con quien hablas ha intentado nada, probablemente el problema no es urgente, por más que lo mencionen. Y separa con cuidado a quien siente el dolor de quien paga. La próxima semana aprenderás métodos de entrevista que hacen estas respuestas todavía más confiables.
