## Learning objectives

- Turn raw interview notes into a synthesis board that separates facts from opinions and shows how strong each finding is.
- Split respondents into segments and write an ideal customer profile based on evidence.
- Identify early adopters using Steve Blank's earlyvangelist traits.
- Decide whether to persevere, adjust or pivot, using the fail lines you set before the interviews.

## Overview

Ten good interviews can still produce a wrong conclusion. Founders remember the vivid story, the friendly person and the quote that confirms what they hoped. Synthesis is the discipline that protects you from your own memory. It turns a pile of notes into a small number of findings, each with a count, a strength and a source. Investors and YC partners can usually tell within minutes whether a team has done this work. The ones who have can say who exactly has the problem, how they cope today and why they will switch. The ones who have not describe their idea.

## From notes to a synthesis board

### Debrief within the hour

Memory decays fast and bends toward what you wanted to hear. After every interview, take ten minutes to write a short debrief: who it was, their role and company size, the key facts, the best quotes in their exact words, the workarounds they described and any commitment they made. Use the same template every time so you can compare later.

### Break notes into single observations

Write each observation on its own card, physical or digital: one fact, one quote or one behavior per card. Tag each card with the person's segment, such as "tech, 60-tech shop" or "service manager, 150-tech shop". Small cards are what make patterns visible. A long paragraph of notes hides them.

### Cluster, then name the clusters

This step comes from affinity mapping, a design-thinking technique taught at the Stanford d.school. Put the cards on a wall or a board and move similar ones together without deciding the categories in advance. Only when the groups have formed do you name them. Good cluster names are statements, not topics: "Techs leave sites without the right part" says more than "Parts".

### Grade the evidence

Not every card is equal. Use a simple three-level scale.

- Strong: observed behavior or a commitment. You saw them do it, they showed you the workaround, or they agreed to a next step.
- Medium: a specific past story with details, told in their words.
- Weak: opinions, predictions, compliments and generic claims.

Then count. For each cluster, how many people, in which segments, at what strength? A finding backed by seven specific stories and three commitments is different from one backed by two enthusiastic opinions, even if both feel convincing.

## Segments and the ideal customer profile

### Look for the segment where the pain is sharpest

Once clusters are counted by segment, differences jump out. One segment may describe the problem every day and already pay for a workaround. Another may agree that it exists but shrug. Your first market is the segment where the pain is sharpest and the path to a purchase is shortest, not the biggest segment on paper.

### Write the ideal customer profile

An ideal customer profile, or ICP, describes the customer you would choose if you could only have one kind. For a business product, it usually covers:

- Company traits: industry, size, location, the tools they already use.
- The trigger: the event or condition that makes the pain urgent right now.
- The workaround: how they cope today and what it costs them.
- The people: who feels the pain, who decides, who pays and who can block.
- Disqualifiers: traits that mean someone is not a fit, even if they are interested.

Every line of an ICP should trace back to cards on your board. If it does not, it is still a hypothesis, and you should label it that way.

## Early adopters

Your first customers will not look like the mainstream market. Steve Blank calls the best of them earlyvangelists, and in "The Four Steps to the Epiphany" he lists their traits. In summary, they:

1. Have a problem.
2. Know they have it.
3. Are actively looking for a solution.
4. Have already pieced together a solution of their own.
5. Have, or can quickly get, a budget for it.

The fourth trait is the most telling in interviews. People who have built a spreadsheet, a group chat, a shared folder or an informal process to cope are showing you, with their own time, that the problem matters. Mark these people on your board. They are the ones to bring into your first MVP test next week.

## Persevere, adjust or pivot

### Go back to your fail lines

In lesson 1 you set a fail line for each hypothesis before the interviews. Now compare. For each hypothesis, write the result next to the line and mark it as confirmed, rejected or unclear. Be honest about unclear results. They usually mean the segment was too broad or the question was too vague, and the fix is more targeted interviews, not a decision.

### What a pivot is and is not

Eric Ries, in "The Lean Startup", describes the pivot as a structured change in one part of the strategy to test a new hypothesis, while keeping what you have already learned. He names several kinds, including a zoom-in, where one feature or part of the problem becomes the whole product, and a customer segment pivot, where you solve a similar problem for a different customer.

YC partners, including Dalton Caldwell in his Startup School talks on pivoting, draw a useful line between a pivot and flailing. A pivot is one deliberate change backed by evidence, often toward what the founders know well. Flailing is a string of unrelated changes made because nothing is working. If you cannot name the evidence behind a change, you are flailing.

### A simple decision rule

- Persevere when your core problem and segment hypotheses passed and you have commitments.
- Adjust when the problem is real but the emphasis is wrong, or one segment clearly outperforms the rest.
- Pivot when the core problem hypothesis failed across segments, or nobody will commit time, reputation or money.

## Case study: Fieldnote

By the end of the week, Ana and Tomás had spoken with 18 people: 10 technicians, 6 service managers and 2 owners. Every number below is the founders' own interview data.

They broke their notes into about 200 cards and clustered them over one long evening. Three clusters dominated.

- Techs leave sites without the right part. 8 of 10 technicians described a job in the last month where they could not identify a part on site. Several showed photos they had sent to senior colleagues at night.
- Repeat visits are the cost managers feel. 6 of the 8 managers and owners named repeat visits, jobs not fixed on the first trip, as a top-three cost, and 4 could pull the numbers from their own records.
- Paperwork is annoying but partly solved. Only 3 of 10 technicians said they had lost more than an hour to paperwork in the last week. Most already used field-service-management software for job reports.

Against their fail lines, the paperwork hypothesis failed: 3 was below the line of 4. The repeat-visit hypothesis passed. The segment hypothesis also held. The two very small shops, with fewer than 10 technicians, had owners who diagnosed hard jobs themselves and felt little pain. One large contractor, with several hundred technicians, had in-house tools and a long procurement process. Shops with 20 to 200 technicians described the problem most sharply and made all 5 commitments.

Their week 2 estimate, that 30 to 40 percent of a visit goes to diagnosis and paperwork, needed revising. The time was real, but it sat mostly in diagnosis and parts lookup, and its biggest cost was the repeat visit, not the report.

They chose to adjust, not pivot. The customer stayed the same. The lead problem became first-time-fix rate and parts lookup. Their new ICP: commercial and residential HVAC contractors with 20 to 200 technicians, a service manager who tracks callbacks, a shortage of senior technicians and an existing field-service tool. Disqualifier: shops where the owner still handles every hard diagnosis. The earlyvangelists were clear: the service managers who already ran night-time photo chats with their senior technicians. In week 4, those five shops become the first MVP test.

## Common mistakes

- Synthesizing from memory instead of from written notes and debriefs.
- Counting every comment the same, so a compliment weighs as much as a commitment.
- Averaging across segments and hiding the one where the pain is sharpest.
- Writing an ICP from the market you want rather than the evidence you have.
- Calling a series of random changes a pivot.

## Key takeaways

- Synthesis turns memories into counted, graded evidence that others can check.
- Name clusters as statements and count them by segment.
- Your first market is the segment where the pain is sharpest and the path to purchase is shortest.
- Earlyvangelists have already built a workaround. Find them and bring them into your first test.
- A pivot is one deliberate change backed by evidence. Anything else is flailing.
