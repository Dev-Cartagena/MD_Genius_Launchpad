## Learning objectives

- Explain why a startup is a search for a business model, not the execution of a plan.
- Describe the four steps of Steve Blank's customer development and where customer discovery fits.
- Turn your riskiest assumptions into hypotheses that a real test could prove wrong.
- Plan a first week of discovery with a target number of conversations and a clear pass or fail line for each hypothesis.

## Overview

AI has made building cheap. A founder with a good model and a weekend can ship something that looks finished. That is why discovery matters more in 2026, not less: the scarce thing is no longer the ability to build, it is knowing what to build and for whom. Steve Blank, who created the Lean LaunchPad, argued in 2026 that a working product no longer proves a team understands its customers. This week you collect that understanding directly, from the people whose problem you want to solve.

## A startup is a search, not a plan

### Blank's definition

Steve Blank defines a startup as an organization formed to search for a repeatable and scalable business model. Each word matters. Search means you do not yet know the answer. Repeatable means you can win the next customer the same way you won the last one. Scalable means each new customer costs you less effort than the revenue they bring. A large company executes a known model. A startup is still looking for one.

This changes what a good week looks like. In a startup, progress is learning: a belief that was a guess on Monday is a fact, or is dead, by Friday.

### Why plans fail first contact

Blank adapted a military saying: no business plan survives first contact with customers. A plan written at a desk is a stack of untested guesses about who buys, why, at what price and through which channel. They are not wrong because the founder is careless. They are wrong because nobody can know them without asking. Blank's shorthand for the fix is "get out of the building."

## Customer development in four steps

Blank's customer development process, first set out in his book "The Four Steps to the Epiphany" and expanded with Bob Dorf in "The Startup Owner's Manual", runs alongside product development. It has four steps.

1. Customer discovery. Turn the founders' vision into a set of hypotheses about the business model, then test them with real people. Does the problem exist, is it painful, and does your proposed solution matter to them?
2. Customer validation. Test whether the model is repeatable. Can you sell to customers who are not your friends, at a real price, through a channel you can grow?
3. Customer creation. Once the model works, spend to build demand at scale.
4. Company building. Move from a searching team to an organization with departments and processes built to execute.

Weeks 3 and 4 of this program live in step one. If discovery fails, you loop back and change a hypothesis. Blank calls that loop a pivot. Moving to customer validation before discovery has produced real evidence is the most common and most expensive mistake in this process.

### The Lean LaunchPad

Blank turned this process into the Lean LaunchPad, a course he first taught at Stanford in 2011 that later ran at Berkeley, many other universities and the US National Science Foundation's I-Corps program. Teams write hypotheses into each box of a business model canvas, talk to many customers every week, then present what changed. Nothing on the canvas counts as true until a customer has confirmed it.

## From assumptions to hypotheses you can break

In week 2 you mapped your riskiest assumptions. An assumption is a belief. A hypothesis is a belief written so that evidence can kill it. Most founders write hypotheses that cannot fail, which means the test teaches them nothing.

### The anatomy of a testable hypothesis

A useful hypothesis names four things:

- Who: a specific kind of person in a specific setting, such as "service managers at commercial HVAC contractors with 20 to 200 technicians".
- What: the behavior or pain you expect to find, such as "spend time every week chasing the right part number".
- How you will check it: the conversation or test you will run.
- The line: the result that would make you abandon or change it, decided before you look.

Compare two versions. Weak: "Technicians want AI help." Nobody can disprove this, so it is useless. Strong: "At least 6 of 10 technicians we interview will describe a job in the last month where they left a site without fixing the equipment because they could not identify a part." That one can fail, and if it fails, you have learned something real.

### Rank by risk, test the scariest first

Not every hypothesis deserves a test this week. Rank them by two questions. If this is wrong, does the company die? How little do we know about it today? Start at the top of both. Customer and problem hypotheses usually come first, because if nobody has the problem, the rest of the canvas does not matter. Pricing, channels and partnerships can wait until you know someone cares.

### Set the line before you look

Write the pass or fail line down before the first conversation. If you set it afterwards, you will set it wherever the data happens to land. This one habit separates evidence from storytelling.

## Planning your first week out of the building

### Who to talk to

Start with people who have the problem now, not people who might have it someday. Use your network for the first five, then widen it: industry forums, trade associations, LinkedIn searches by job title, local meetups, suppliers who sell to your target customers. Ask every person you speak to for two more names. Introductions convert far better than cold messages.

Talk to more than one role. In business software, the person who feels the pain, the person who pays and the person who can block the purchase are often different. You need all three views before you can claim a hypothesis holds.

### How many conversations

There is no magic number, and you should be suspicious of anyone who gives you one as a law. YC partners commonly advise founders to keep talking to users until they stop hearing new things. A practical target for this week is ten to fifteen conversations with your core segment. Patterns usually start to appear by the eighth or tenth. If after fifteen you still hear something different every time, your segment is too broad.

### Go where the work happens

A phone call tells you what someone remembers. A ride-along or a screen share shows you what they actually do, including the small repeated decisions an AI could take over.

## Case study: Fieldnote

At the end of week 2, Ana and Tomás had a problem statement: field technicians lose about 30 to 40 percent of a visit to diagnosis and paperwork. They knew this number was their own estimate, drawn from Ana's years running HVAC operations. This week they turned it into hypotheses they could break.

They wrote three, each with a line set in advance.

1. Problem: at least 6 of 10 technicians will describe losing more than an hour in the last week to paperwork after jobs. Fail line: fewer than 4.
2. Problem: at least half of service managers will name repeat visits, jobs not fixed on the first trip, as a top-three cost. Fail line: fewer than a third.
3. Segment: contractors with 20 to 200 technicians feel the pain more sharply than very small shops, because they have enough volume for it to hurt and too few senior technicians to cover every call.

They split the work. Ana used her network for the first introductions, called two HVAC distributors and asked a regional contractors' association to share a short note. Tomás arranged two ride-alongs so he could watch a diagnosis on site instead of hearing about it. Each asked every person for two more names.

Over the following days they would speak with 18 people: technicians, service managers and two owners. The results of that work are the subject of lesson 3. The lesson for now is how they started: with specific people, specific claims and a line drawn before the first call.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the first version of the business model as a plan to execute rather than a list of guesses to test.
- Writing hypotheses so vague that no result could disprove them.
- Setting the pass or fail line after seeing the data.
- Talking only to friends, investors or people who will never buy.
- Letting one co-founder do all the discovery while the other builds, so only one of you holds the evidence.

## Key takeaways

- A startup is a temporary organization searching for a repeatable, scalable business model.
- Customer discovery is the first of Steve Blank's four steps, and it must produce evidence before you scale anything.
- A hypothesis is only useful if a real result could prove it wrong.
- Decide the fail line before you look at the data.
- Go to where the work happens, and keep talking until you stop hearing new things.
