## Learning objectives

- Apply the three rules of Rob Fitzpatrick's The Mom Test to write interview questions that produce facts, not compliments.
- Recognize bad data in an interview, including compliments, generic claims, hypotheticals and feature requests, and steer back to specifics.
- Ask for commitment and advancement so you can tell real interest from politeness.
- Recruit interviewees who have the problem, and run a 30-minute interview with a clear goal.

## Overview

An interview can make you more wrong than you were before it. People are kind, they want to help, and they do not want to tell a stranger that the idea is weak. If you ask whether they would use your product, most will say yes, and you will leave with false confidence. Rob Fitzpatrick named the fix in his book "The Mom Test": ask questions so grounded in someone's real life that even your mother could not lie to you while answering them. This lesson turns that idea into a practical method you can use this week.

## The Mom Test in three rules

Fitzpatrick's test is simple. A good customer question is one that even someone who loves you cannot answer with a polite lie. He gives three rules to get there.

### Talk about their life, not your idea

The moment you describe your idea, the conversation changes. The other person starts evaluating you instead of describing their world. Hold your idea back as long as you can. Ask how they do the job today, what is annoying about it and what they have tried. You learn more from ten minutes of their workday than from an hour of opinions about your product.

### Ask about specifics in the past, not opinions about the future

"Would you use this?" invites a guess about a future that has not happened. "How often does that happen?" invites an average they will invent on the spot. Instead ask about a real recent event: "Tell me about the last time this happened. What did you do next?" Past behavior is evidence. Future intentions are hopes.

### Talk less and listen more

If you are talking, you are not learning. Aim for the other person to speak most of the time. Ask a short question, then wait. Silence feels uncomfortable for you and is often where the most useful detail arrives. Write down exact phrases they use. Their words will later become your landing page and your sales pitch.

## Questions that work

Eric Migicovsky, founder of Pebble and a YC partner, teaches a short set of questions in his Startup School talk "How to Talk to Users" that fit the Mom Test well. In our own words, they run like this:

1. What is the hardest part of doing this job today?
2. Tell me about the last time you ran into that problem.
3. Why was it hard?
4. What, if anything, have you done to solve it?
5. What do you dislike about the solutions you have tried?

Each answer opens a follow-up. If someone says a problem is painful but has never tried to solve it, not with a spreadsheet, a workaround or a paid tool, the problem may be real but not important. People who are actively hacking a fix are your best early signal.

### Dig one level deeper

The best answers come from the second and third follow-up. "Why does that matter to you?" "What did that cost you?" "Who else was involved?" "Can you show me?" When someone gives you a number, ask how they know it. When they say "always", ask for the last specific time.

### Recognizing bad data

Fitzpatrick lists three kinds of bad data that feel like progress and are not.

- Compliments. "That sounds great" tells you nothing. Deflect it and return to their life: "Thanks. How are you handling this today?"
- Fluff. Generic claims ("I usually"), future promises ("I would definitely") and hypotheticals ("I might"). Anchor them in a real event: "When was the last time you did that?"
- Ideas and feature requests. These are useful, but do not add them to the roadmap on the spot. Ask why they want it, what it would let them do and how they cope without it. The motivation behind the request is the real data.

A simple test after each call: could you repeat a specific story they told you, with names, numbers and what happened next? If not, you collected opinions.

## Commitment and advancement

Kind words cost nothing. To know whether someone is serious, ask them to give something up. Fitzpatrick describes three currencies of commitment:

- Time: a follow-up meeting with a clear agenda, a pilot they will actually use, a session where they share real data.
- Reputation: an introduction to their boss, a peer or the person who signs contracts.
- Money: a pre-order, a deposit, a letter of intent or a paid pilot.

Advancement means the relationship moves one concrete step closer to a sale. If a meeting ends with "this is great, keep me posted", it went nowhere, however warm it felt. End every serious conversation by asking for a next step. A clear no is useful. A vague yes is the most misleading result you can get.

## Recruiting and running the interview

### Finding people

Go where your customers already gather: industry associations, trade forums, supplier counters, LinkedIn by job title, local meetups. Warm introductions beat cold outreach. When you write cold, keep it short and honest:

1. Who you are, in one line.
2. The problem you are studying, not the product you are building.
3. Why you are asking them in particular.
4. A small, specific ask: twenty minutes, this week.

Frame it as learning, not selling. Fitzpatrick also advises keeping conversations casual when you can. A chat at a trade event or a site visit often yields more truth than a formal "interview" with a calendar invite.

### Before, during and after

Before each call, write down the three things you most want to learn from this person. During the call, one founder asks and one takes notes, verbatim where possible. Record only with permission. After the call, spend ten minutes writing up the key facts, quotes and the commitment you asked for, while it is fresh. Lesson 3 shows how to turn those notes into evidence.

### Interviews for AI products

AI startups face a specific trap. People are curious about AI and will happily talk about it, which is not the same as having a problem you can solve. Keep the conversation on the work, not the technology. Two extra questions help: "When does a wrong answer here cost you something, and how much?" and "What data or documents do you check today to get this right?" The first tells you how accurate you must be. The second tells you what your system will need access to.

## Case study: Fieldnote

Ana's first three interviews went badly, and she knew it. She described the Fieldnote idea in the first minute. Technicians said it sounded useful and that they would try it. She left each call with nothing she could act on.

Tomás rewrote the guide around the Mom Test. The new opening was: "Walk me through the last service call you could not close on the first visit." That one question changed the interviews. A technician in a 60-person shop described a rooftop unit with a failed compressor contactor. He had photographed the nameplate, called the supply house, waited on hold, and still guessed wrong about the replacement part. He drove back two days later. When Ana asked what that cost, the service manager on the next call put a number on it from her own records: each repeat visit cost the shop most of a technician's half-day, plus the customer's patience.

Paperwork came up too, but people described it as a nuisance they had already partly solved with their field-service software. Parts and diagnosis were where they described real workarounds: photos sent to senior technicians at night, personal notebooks of model numbers, calls to a retired colleague.

The founders also tested commitment. At the end of strong calls, Ana asked managers whether they would put two technicians on a four-week trial and share their callback data. Five of the eight managers and owners they spoke to said yes and named a start date. Several others said "send me something", which the founders logged as no commitment.

## Common mistakes

- Pitching the idea in the first minutes, which turns the interview into a sales call.
- Asking "would you use" or "would you pay" and trusting the answer.
- Accepting averages and generalities instead of asking for the last specific time.
- Leaving a warm conversation without asking for any next step.
- Interviewing people who find the topic interesting but do not have the problem.

## Your turn

Write a one-page interview guide for your next conversation. Include a two-sentence opening that does not mention your product, five past-focused questions, two follow-up probes, and one commitment ask that fits your stage. Then review last week's notes from any earlier conversations and mark each line as fact, compliment, fluff or idea. Spend 20 to 30 minutes.

## Key takeaways

- A good question is one that even a kind person cannot answer with a polite lie.
- Ask about specific past events, not future intentions or opinions.
- Compliments, fluff and feature requests are not evidence until you dig beneath them.
- Real interest shows up as commitment of time, reputation or money, and as advancement to a next step.
- For AI products, keep the interview on the work and learn the cost of a wrong answer.
