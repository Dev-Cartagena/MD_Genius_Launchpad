# Video: Interviews that tell the truth
Duration: ~4:00 · Format: 16:9 1920×1080 · Presenter: Maya Ortiz — calm, mid-30s Latin American founder-turned-investor, navy blazer, neutral studio

## Scene 1 — Hook (0:00–0:25)
- Type: presenter
- On screen: Maya centered, medium shot. Lower third: "Week 3 · Lesson 2". Title fades in at right in Space Grotesk, Navy: "Interviews that tell the truth".
- Narration EN: Here is an uncomfortable fact. A customer interview can leave you more wrong than you were before it. People are kind. They want to help. And if you ask whether they would use your product, most of them will say yes. In this lesson you learn how to ask questions that even kind people cannot answer with a polite lie.
- Narración ES: Aquí va un hecho incómodo. Una entrevista con un cliente puede alejarte de la verdad más de lo que estabas antes. La gente es amable. Quiere ayudar. Y si le preguntas si usaría tu producto, la mayoría dirá que sí. En esta lección aprendes a hacer preguntas que ni siquiera una persona amable puede responder con una mentira cortés.

## Scene 2 — The Mom Test (0:25–0:55)
- Type: motion
- On screen: White Smoke background. Heading "The Mom Test" with small credit "Rob Fitzpatrick". Three numbered rules build in sequence, each with a Steel underline: "1. Talk about their life, not your idea", "2. Ask about specifics in the past, not the future", "3. Talk less, listen more". A small speech-bubble meter at the bottom fills to about three quarters on the interviewee side.
- Narration EN: Rob Fitzpatrick calls it the Mom Test: ask questions so grounded in someone's real life that even your mother could not lie to you. He gives three rules. Talk about their life, not your idea, because once you pitch, they start judging you instead of describing their world. Ask about specific events in the past, not opinions about the future. And talk less. If you are talking, you are not learning.
- Narración ES: Rob Fitzpatrick lo llama The Mom Test: haz preguntas tan ancladas en la vida real de la otra persona que ni siquiera tu mamá podría mentirte. Propone tres reglas. Habla de su vida, no de tu idea, porque en cuanto presentas, empieza a juzgarte en lugar de describir su mundo. Pregunta por hechos específicos del pasado, no por opiniones sobre el futuro. Y habla menos. Si tú hablas, no estás aprendiendo.

## Scene 3 — Questions that work (0:55–1:20)
- Type: split
- On screen: Left, Maya. Right, a vertical list of five questions appearing one at a time in Navy on White Smoke: "What is the hardest part of this job?", "Tell me about the last time.", "Why was it hard?", "What have you tried?", "What do you dislike about it?". Small credit: "After Eric Migicovsky, YC Startup School".
- Narration EN: Eric Migicovsky, the founder of Pebble, teaches a short set of questions at YC's Startup School. What is the hardest part of this job? Tell me about the last time it happened. Why was it hard? What have you tried? What do you dislike about those solutions? Listen closely to that fourth answer. People who are already hacking together a fix are your strongest early signal.
- Narración ES: Eric Migicovsky, fundador de Pebble, enseña en Startup School de YC un conjunto breve de preguntas. ¿Qué es lo más difícil de este trabajo? Cuéntame de la última vez que pasó. ¿Por qué fue difícil? ¿Qué has intentado? ¿Qué no te gusta de esas soluciones? Escucha con atención la cuarta respuesta. Quienes ya improvisan una solución son tu señal temprana más fuerte.

## Scene 4 — Bad data (1:20–1:50)
- Type: motion
- On screen: Three cards labeled "Compliments", "Fluff", "Ideas" drop in, each in Mist with a short example below: "That sounds great", "I would definitely use it", "You should add a dashboard". Each card then flips to a Navy side showing the redirect: "How do you handle it today?", "When did you last do that?", "Why do you want that?".
- Narration EN: Watch for three kinds of bad data. Compliments, like "that sounds great", tell you nothing. Fluff, like "I would definitely use it", is a guess about the future. Feature requests are useful, but the real data is the reason behind them. Each one has a fix: steer back to a real event in their life. How do you handle it today? When did you last do that? Why do you want it?
- Narración ES: Cuidado con tres tipos de datos malos. Los cumplidos, como "suena genial", no te dicen nada. El relleno, como "seguro lo usaría", es una suposición sobre el futuro. Los pedidos de funciones son útiles, pero el dato real es la razón detrás de ellos. Cada uno tiene solución: vuelve a un hecho real de su vida. ¿Cómo lo resuelves hoy? ¿Cuándo lo hiciste por última vez? ¿Por qué lo quieres?

## Scene 5 — Commitment and advancement (1:50–2:20)
- Type: motion
- On screen: Three coins labeled "Time", "Reputation", "Money" line up left to right, each with a one-line example beneath: "a trial with a start date", "an introduction to the boss", "a deposit or paid pilot". Below, a horizontal path with a marker that advances one step when a coin is placed, labeled "Advancement". A grey speech bubble "Keep me posted" sits off the path, labeled "no commitment".
- Narration EN: Kind words cost nothing. To know if someone is serious, ask them to give something up. Fitzpatrick names three currencies: time, reputation and money. A trial with a start date. An introduction to the person who signs. A deposit. End every serious conversation by asking for a next step. A clear no is useful. A vague yes, like "keep me posted", is the most misleading result you can get.
- Narración ES: Las palabras amables no cuestan nada. Para saber si alguien va en serio, pídele que entregue algo. Fitzpatrick nombra tres monedas: tiempo, reputación y dinero. Una prueba con fecha de inicio. Una presentación con quien firma. Un depósito. Cierra cada conversación seria pidiendo un siguiente paso. Un no claro es útil. Un sí vago, como "mantenme al tanto", es el resultado más engañoso que puedes obtener.

## Scene 6 — Recruiting interviewees (2:20–2:55)
- Type: motion
- On screen: A short message card builds in four numbered lines on White Smoke: "1. Who you are", "2. The problem you study", "3. Why you", "4. Twenty minutes this week". A Steel tag reads "learning, not selling". Beside it, a small icon row: "Associations", "Forums", "Supply counters", "Site visits".
- Narration EN: Now, finding people. Go where your customers already gather: associations, trade forums, supplier counters, job-title searches. Warm introductions beat cold messages. When you do write cold, keep it to four lines: who you are, the problem you are studying, not the product, why you are asking this person, and a small ask, twenty minutes this week. Frame it as learning, not selling. And when you can, keep it casual. A chat at a trade event often yields more truth than a formal interview.
- Narración ES: Ahora, cómo encontrar personas. Ve a donde tus clientes ya se reúnen: asociaciones, foros del sector, mostradores de proveedores, búsquedas por cargo. Las presentaciones cálidas superan a los mensajes en frío. Cuando escribas en frío, usa cuatro líneas: quién eres, el problema que estudias, no el producto, por qué le escribes a esa persona y un pedido pequeño, veinte minutos esta semana. Plantéalo como aprendizaje, no como venta. Y cuando puedas, que sea informal. Una charla en un evento del sector suele dar más verdad que una entrevista formal.
